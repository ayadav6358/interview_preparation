1. What is this?

The orientation session for the 100x AI & ML course, delivered by Rishabh (Research Engineer, previously at an AI lab in London working on LLMs and LLM-RL, with a NeurIPS 2025 workshop paper accepted — "Loong: Synthesize Long Chain-of-Thoughts at Scale through Verifiers"). It states the course's goals, what to prepare before starting, and a couple of free tools worth exploring alongside the course.

2. Why does it exist?

AI/LLM engineering moves fast enough that a course needs to explicitly set expectations up front: this isn't a one-time static curriculum, it's a field where "new things come everyday" — so orientation exists to align learners on mindset (adaptability, intuition over heavy math) before diving into technical material.

3. Why is it important?

Setting the right expectations early avoids two common failure modes in AI learning: (1) treating the field as a fixed body of facts to memorize instead of a fast-moving practice requiring continuous updating, and (2) getting stuck on math/theory instead of building practical, working intuition — which is explicitly the pedagogical choice this course makes.

4. Where is it used?

This orientation format — goals, instructor background, prerequisites, "how to stay current" — is standard at the start of any serious technical bootcamp/course track, especially in fast-moving fields like AI engineering, security, or cloud infrastructure.

5. Prerequisites

Basic Python, a Google Colab account (for running notebooks with free GPU access), and curiosity to explore AI models/products already on the market before class starts.

9. How the course approach works

The course deliberately minimizes heavy mathematical derivation in favor of intuition-first explanations, paired with hands-on coding (PyTorch, Colab) — the goal is a practitioner who can reason about and build with LLMs, not necessarily derive their math from first principles. This mirrors how most working AI engineers actually operate day to day.

10. Step-by-step: how to prepare

  1. Refresh basic Python fluency (functions, loops, classes, list/dict operations) if rusty.
  2. Create and verify access to a Google Colab account, including confirming free GPU runtime access.
  3. Spend time exploring a few different AI models/products currently on the market (e.g., different chat assistants, coding tools) to build intuition for the landscape before the technical material starts.
  4. Install/try at least one free AI coding tool (Opencode or Gemini CLI) to get hands-on early.
  5. Treat the course as an ongoing practice, not a one-time unit — plan to keep following new developments after the course ends.

14. Advanced example (staying current)

Setting up a lightweight personal "staying current" pipeline: following a curated list of AI researchers/labs on X/Twitter, subscribing to a weekly AI newsletter, periodically skimming new arXiv papers in your area of interest (e.g., via arXiv-sanity or Hugging Face's daily papers page), and trying new open-source models/tools as they release rather than reading about them secondhand.

15. Common mistakes

  • Treating this as a one-time course to "finish" rather than an entry point into continuous learning — the field genuinely changes week to week.
  • Getting stuck trying to master deep mathematical derivations before writing any code, when the course's explicit approach favors intuition + hands-on building.
  • Skipping the "explore models already on the market" step — hands-on familiarity with existing products builds essential intuition before internals are covered.
  • Not setting up Colab/GPU access in advance, causing friction once hands-on coding sessions start.

16. Best practices

  • Do the "explore models in the market" homework seriously — try multiple chat assistants, coding tools, and note what differs between them.
  • Keep a running list of terms/concepts you don't yet understand instead of getting stuck on them mid-session.
  • Build a lightweight habit (even 10–15 minutes/week) for tracking new AI releases and papers, rather than relying solely on the course.
  • Prioritize working code over perfect theoretical understanding, especially early on — intuition deepens through building.

20. Cost considerations

The course deliberately points students to free tooling to remove cost as a barrier to entry: Google Colab (free GPU tier), Opencode, and Gemini CLI — all usable at zero cost for learning and experimentation before any need to pay for compute or paid APIs arises.

21. Debugging/troubleshooting

  • Colab GPU not available: check Runtime → Change runtime type → Hardware accelerator is set to GPU; free-tier GPU availability can also be rate-limited/queued during high-demand periods.
  • Unsure which AI tool to explore first: start with whichever is free and has the lowest setup friction (Opencode or Gemini CLI, as suggested) rather than overthinking the choice.
  • Feeling overwhelmed by "new things every day": this is explicitly named as normal/expected in the orientation — the goal is building an ongoing-learning habit, not catching up to a finish line.

28. Interview Questions — Intermediate

Q1. Why might an intuition-first, low-math teaching approach be a deliberate pedagogical trade-off rather than a shortcut?
A. For most practicing AI engineers, day-to-day work is about building, debugging, and reasoning about systems (prompting, fine-tuning, architecture choices) rather than deriving backpropagation from scratch — prioritizing working intuition and hands-on skill gets learners to practical competence faster, while deep math can be layered in later for those who need it (e.g., for research roles).
Q2. Why does the orientation explicitly frame "staying up to date" as an ongoing goal rather than a one-time course outcome?
A. Because AI/LLM engineering is unusually fast-moving even compared to other software fields — new models, techniques, and tools ship on a near-weekly cadence, so a static curriculum alone would quickly become outdated; the course is positioning itself as teaching a learning habit/process, not just a fixed body of facts.

29. Interview Questions — Advanced

Q1. How would you design a personal system to stay current in AI engineering without it becoming a full-time, unsustainable habit?
A. Time-box it (e.g., 15-30 minutes weekly) using 1-2 curated, high-signal sources rather than many noisy ones, filter aggressively for relevance to your current work/learning track rather than trying to absorb everything, and prefer trying new tools hands-on over just reading about them — hands-on exploration builds retained intuition far faster than passive reading, and is more sustainable than attempting comprehensive coverage of a field moving this fast.

30. Interview Questions — Senior/Architect

Q1. As a technical lead onboarding a team into AI engineering, how would you balance teaching deep theoretical foundations (math, architecture internals) against practical, ship-fast intuition, given how quickly the field changes?
A. Sequence it deliberately: start with intuition-first, hands-on exposure (mirroring this orientation's approach) to build working competence and genuine interest quickly, then layer deeper theoretical grounding (attention math, training dynamics, optimization theory) for engineers who'll own architecture decisions, debugging of subtle model behavior, or research-adjacent work — not uniformly for the whole team. Pair this with an explicit, lightweight team habit for tracking new developments (a shared weekly notes channel, rotating "what's new" presentations) so the team's practical knowledge doesn't silently decay as the field moves, while avoiding over-investing engineering time in chasing every new release.

31. Scenario-Based Questions

Scenario 1. A new team member feels overwhelmed because "something new in AI" seems to ship every single day, and they're worried about falling behind. How do you coach them?
A. Normalize it explicitly — this is a stated, expected characteristic of the field (as this very orientation calls out), not a sign they're behind; redirect their energy toward depth on fundamentals (which change slowly) plus a small, sustainable weekly habit for surface-level awareness of new developments, rather than trying to comprehensively track everything released daily.
Scenario 2. A learner wants to skip the "explore existing AI models/products" homework and jump straight into the technical material. Why would you push back?
A. Hands-on familiarity with existing products builds intuition for what "good" AI behavior looks like, what current capabilities/limitations are, and what problems are already solved — without that grounding, technical internals (attention, training) land as abstract concepts disconnected from what they're actually used to build; the exploration step is cheap and compounds the value of everything that follows.
