    Embeddings and Vector Databases

    ⏱ ~2.5 hrs📍 Chapter 3 of 7

    One-line summary: An embedding turns text into a fixed-length vector such that semantically similar text ends up geometrically close, and a vector database is just a specialized index (HNSW, IVF, or brute force) for finding "nearest" vectors fast at scale — understanding both is what makes RAG an engineering decision instead of a magic black box.

    On this page

      Why It Matters for a Senior Developer

      Every RAG system, semantic search feature, and recommendation-by-similarity feature you'll be asked about in an interview rests on this layer. The questions that separate someone who read a vector-DB product's marketing page from someone who understands it are precise: what does cosine similarity actually measure versus Euclidean distance; why does an approximate nearest-neighbor index trade recall for speed, and by how much; why do embedding dimensions and model choice matter for both cost and quality; why does "semantic search returned irrelevant results" usually trace back to a chunking or embedding-model mismatch rather than a vector-DB bug. You should be able to design this layer with the same rigor you'd apply to picking a database index strategy, because that's exactly what it is.

      Why

      Vector search is "just" nearest-neighbor search over high-dimensional points — a well-understood CS problem with real algorithmic tradeoffs, not a mysterious AI capability. Treating it that way is what lets you debug a bad-retrieval incident systematically instead of randomly swapping libraries.

      Prerequisites

      Basic vector/linear algebra intuition (dot product, distance) and familiarity with database indexing concepts help. Related: How LLMs Work, RAG.

      Core Concepts

      What an embedding actually is

      An embedding model is a neural network (often a smaller transformer encoder, sometimes derived from the same family as chat LLMs) trained so that its output vector — typically 384 to 3072 floating-point numbers, depending on the model — captures the meaning of the input text, such that texts with similar meaning produce vectors that are close together in that high-dimensional space, and dissimilar texts produce vectors that are far apart. Training typically uses contrastive objectives: pairs of texts known to be related (a question and its correct answer passage, two paraphrases) are pulled together in vector space, while unrelated pairs are pushed apart, across massive amounts of data. The vector itself has no human-readable meaning per dimension — you cannot point to dimension 47 and say what it represents — the meaning only exists in the geometric relationships between many vectors.

      Cosine similarity vs. Euclidean distance vs. dot product

      Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors, ignoring their magnitude: cos(θ) = (A · B) / (‖A‖ ‖B‖), ranging from -1 (opposite direction) to 1 (identical direction). Most text embedding models are trained and intended to be compared this way, because what matters is the direction of meaning, not the vector's length (which can vary with text length or model idiosyncrasies and carries no semantic signal). Euclidean distance measures straight-line distance between the two points, which is sensitive to magnitude — two vectors pointing the same direction but different lengths register as "far apart" even though cosine similarity would call them nearly identical. Dot product (unnormalized) combines both angle and magnitude; if embeddings are pre-normalized to unit length (many models' outputs are, or you normalize them yourself), dot product and cosine similarity become mathematically equivalent and dot product is cheaper to compute (no division), which is why many vector DBs default to dot product under the hood for "cosine" similarity once you've confirmed normalization. Picking the wrong metric for a model not trained/intended for it is a real, silent cause of degraded retrieval quality.

      Exact vs. approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search

      Exact nearest-neighbor search (brute-force) compares the query vector against every stored vector and returns the true top-k closest — perfectly accurate (100% recall) but O(n) per query, which becomes too slow once you have more than roughly tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand vectors depending on dimensionality and latency budget. Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) algorithms trade a small amount of recall (the fraction of true nearest neighbors actually found) for dramatically better speed and scalability, by building an index structure that avoids comparing against every single vector.

      HNSW: Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs

      HNSW is the dominant ANN algorithm in modern vector databases (pgvector, Qdrant, Weaviate, Pinecone all support it). It builds a multi-layer graph: the top layer has very few nodes with long-range connections (for fast coarse navigation across the whole space), and each layer below has progressively more nodes with shorter-range, denser connections, down to the bottom layer containing every vector. A search starts at an entry point in the top layer, greedily moves to the neighbor closest to the query at that layer, and once no closer neighbor exists at that layer, drops down to the next layer and repeats — like using a highway system to get near your destination fast, then dropping to local streets to find the exact address. This gives logarithmic-ish search time instead of linear, at the cost of: build time (inserting a vector requires finding its place in this graph structure, which is more expensive than just appending to a list), and tunable recall via parameters like ef_search (how many candidates to explore per layer during query — higher means better recall but slower queries) and M (how many connections each node keeps — higher means better recall and more memory, built once at index-construction time). HNSW has no inherent support for deleting vectors cheaply — deletions are typically handled with tombstone marking or periodic rebuilds, a real operational consideration for data that changes often.

      IVF: Inverted File Index

      IVF clusters the vector space into n_list partitions (via k-means, typically), assigning each stored vector to its nearest cluster centroid. A query first finds the n_probe closest centroids to itself, then only searches the vectors within those clusters, rather than the whole dataset — conceptually similar to a database's coarse partition pruning. IVF generally has faster build/insert time than HNSW and lower memory overhead, but is typically less accurate at a given speed than HNSW (recall/speed tradeoff curve is worse) and is sensitive to cluster quality — if the data distribution shifts significantly after clusters are built, recall degrades until the index is rebuilt. IVF is often combined with product quantization (IVF-PQ) to compress vectors and support billion-scale datasets with bounded memory, at a further, deliberate accuracy cost.

      Choosing between brute force, HNSW, and IVF

      Rule of thumb: under roughly 50k-100k vectors, brute-force (exact) search is often fast enough and removes an entire class of recall-tuning complexity — don't reach for ANN prematurely. Beyond that, HNSW is the default choice for most RAG/search workloads needing good recall at low query latency and able to afford its memory/build-time cost. IVF (or IVF-PQ) becomes attractive at very large scale (tens of millions+ vectors) where HNSW's memory footprint becomes prohibitive and some recall can be traded for dramatically lower memory and faster rebuilds. Pre-filtering (restricting the candidate set by metadata before or during vector search, e.g. "only this tenant's documents") is a feature that differs significantly in implementation quality across vector DBs and is worth explicitly testing, not assuming.

      Choosing an embedding model

      Trade-offs to weigh: dimensionality (higher dimensions generally capture more nuance but cost more storage and slightly more compute per comparison — many modern models offer configurable/truncatable dimensions, e.g. via Matryoshka representation learning, letting you shrink vectors with graceful quality degradation instead of a cliff); context length the embedding model accepts (embedding a whole long document at once loses granularity versus embedding chunks, covered in RAG); domain fit (a general-purpose embedding model may underperform a domain-tuned one for, e.g., legal or code search — code-specific embedding models exist and matter for codebase search); and whether you need multilingual support. Critically: you cannot mix embeddings from different models in the same index — vectors from different models live in unrelated geometric spaces, so comparing a query embedded with model A against documents embedded with model B produces meaningless similarity scores. Changing embedding models requires re-embedding your entire corpus, not just new documents.

      How It Works Under the Hood

      End-to-end path for a semantic search / RAG retrieval query:

      1. Ingestion time: source documents are chunked (see RAG), each chunk is sent to an embedding API/model, producing a fixed-length vector per chunk, stored alongside metadata (source, position, timestamps) in the vector database, which builds/updates its ANN index incrementally or in batches.
      2. Query time: the user's query text is embedded with the same embedding model used at ingestion time, producing a query vector in the same space.
      3. The vector DB's ANN index (HNSW/IVF) is searched for the top-k vectors closest to the query vector by the chosen similarity metric, optionally pre-filtered by metadata (tenant, date range, document type).
      4. The matching chunks' original text (and metadata) are fetched and returned — the vector DB usually stores or references the raw text alongside the vector for this purpose.
      5. (In RAG) these chunks are optionally re-ranked by a more expensive, more accurate model before being inserted into the LLM prompt as grounding context.

      Code Examples

      // Computing cosine similarity manually — useful for debugging a vector DB's results
// against ground truth, or for small-scale brute-force search without a dedicated DB.
function dot(a: number[], b: number[]): number {
  let s = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < a.length; i++) s += a[i] * b[i];
  return s;
}
function norm(a: number[]): number {
  return Math.sqrt(dot(a, a));
}
function cosineSimilarity(a: number[], b: number[]): number {
  return dot(a, b) / (norm(a) * norm(b));
}

// Brute-force top-k search — correct baseline to validate an ANN index's recall against.
function bruteForceTopK(query: number[], corpus: { id: string; vector: number[] }[], k: number) {
  return corpus
    .map((item) => ({ id: item.id, score: cosineSimilarity(query, item.vector) }))
    .sort((a, b) => b.score - a.score)
    .slice(0, k);
}

// pgvector example: HNSW index + cosine distance query (Postgres + pgvector extension).
// Note: pgvector's "<=>" operator is cosine DISTANCE (1 - cosine similarity), so ORDER BY ASC.
/*
CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS vector;

CREATE TABLE chunks (
  id SERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
  content TEXT NOT NULL,
  source TEXT NOT NULL,
  embedding VECTOR(1536) NOT NULL
);

CREATE INDEX ON chunks USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops)
  WITH (m = 16, ef_construction = 64);

-- Query: find the 8 closest chunks to a query embedding.
SELECT id, content, source, 1 - (embedding <=> $1) AS similarity
FROM chunks
ORDER BY embedding <=> $1
LIMIT 8;
*/

// Embedding + upsert pipeline in Node, using an embeddings API.
import OpenAI from "openai";
const openai = new OpenAI();

async function embedAndStore(chunks: { id: string; text: string; source: string }[], db: any) {
  const resp = await openai.embeddings.create({
    model: "text-embedding-3-small", // 1536 dims; keep this model pinned across your whole corpus
    input: chunks.map((c) => c.text),
  });
  for (let i = 0; i < chunks.length; i++) {
    await db.query(
      `INSERT INTO chunks (id, content, source, embedding) VALUES ($1, $2, $3, $4)
       ON CONFLICT (id) DO UPDATE SET embedding = $4, content = $2`,
      [chunks[i].id, chunks[i].text, chunks[i].source, resp.data[i].embedding]
    );
  }
}

// BAD: mixing embedding models across ingestion and query (or old vs new corpus data)
// produces meaningless similarity scores — this is a common, hard-to-notice bug after
// a "model upgrade" that only touched the query path.
async function badSearch(query: string) {
  const qVec = await embedWith("text-embedding-3-large", query); // different model/dims!
  return bruteForceTopK(qVec, corpusEmbeddedWithOldModel, 5);     // silently wrong results

      Real-World Production Scenario at Scale

      At scale

      A docs-search team upgrades their embedding model for "better quality" and only re-embeds new documents going forward to save cost, leaving the existing 500k-document corpus on the old model. Search quality doesn't improve — it gets quietly worse, because queries (embedded with the new model) are now being compared against a corpus that's a mix of two incompatible vector spaces, and cosine similarity between vectors from different models is meaningless noise, not a graceful degradation. The fix required a full re-embedding of the entire corpus (a real cost and multi-hour batch job at that scale) and, going forward, a versioned embedding pipeline where the vector DB schema records which model+version produced each vector, so a future migration can be staged and validated (dual-write old+new, compare recall, cut over) instead of silently mixed.

      Common Mistakes, Gotchas and Best Practices

      Gotcha

      Assuming ANN index recall is "basically 100% in practice" without measuring it. HNSW with poorly tuned ef_search/M, or IVF with too few probed clusters, can silently miss genuinely relevant results — and because the search still returns some plausible-looking results, this failure is invisible unless you measure recall against a known-correct brute-force baseline on a sample.

      • Never compare vectors from different embedding models/versions — re-embed the whole corpus on any model change, and track model+version as metadata per vector.
      • Confirm which similarity metric a vector DB actually computes for "cosine" (true cosine vs. dot-product-assuming-normalization) — results differ if your vectors aren't normalized and the DB assumes they are.
      • Measure ANN recall periodically against a brute-force baseline on a representative sample — don't just trust default index parameters.
      • Be deliberate about metadata pre-filtering performance — some vector DBs apply filters after ANN search (can return too few/irrelevant results if the filter is restrictive) rather than during it.
      • Don't embed enormous documents as a single vector "for simplicity" — a single embedding for a 50-page document smears together many distinct topics, diluting retrieval precision (see chunking in RAG).

      Interview Questions

      Basic

      What is a text embedding, in one sentence?

      A fixed-length vector of numbers produced by a model such that texts with similar meaning produce vectors that are close together in that vector space, and dissimilar texts produce vectors that are far apart.

      Follow-up: can you interpret what any single dimension of the vector "means"?
      What does cosine similarity measure, and why is it preferred over Euclidean distance for most text embeddings?

      It measures the angle between two vectors, ignoring magnitude. Most text embedding models are trained to encode meaning in direction rather than length, so comparing by angle (cosine) matches how the model was trained and intended to be used, whereas Euclidean distance would be sensitive to magnitude differences that carry no semantic signal.

      Follow-up: under what condition does dot product become equivalent to cosine similarity?
      What is the difference between exact and approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search?

      Exact search compares the query against every stored vector and returns the true closest ones (100% recall, but O(n), slow at scale). ANN search uses an index structure (like HNSW or IVF) to avoid comparing against everything, trading a small amount of recall for much better speed and scalability.

      Follow-up: at roughly what corpus size does it usually make sense to move from exact to approximate search?
      Can you mix vectors from two different embedding models in the same similarity search?

      No — different models produce vectors in unrelated geometric spaces, so comparing them produces meaningless similarity scores. The same model and version must be used for both the corpus and the query, and changing models requires re-embedding the whole corpus.

      Follow-up: how would you stage a safe migration to a new embedding model without downtime or a quality regression?
      What role does a vector database play beyond "storing vectors"?

      It builds and maintains an index (often HNSW or IVF) over the stored vectors to make nearest-neighbor queries fast at scale, typically also storing associated metadata and supporting filtered search, and handles the operational concerns (updates, deletes, persistence) around that index.

      Follow-up: why might a plain Postgres table with pgvector be a reasonable choice instead of a dedicated vector DB product?

      Medium

      Explain HNSW's search mechanism at a level you could whiteboard.

      HNSW builds a multi-layer graph: sparse, long-range connections at the top layer for fast coarse navigation, denser short-range connections at lower layers, with every vector present at the bottom layer. A query starts at an entry point in the top layer, greedily walks to the closest neighbor at that layer until no closer neighbor exists, then drops to the next layer down and repeats, refining toward the true nearest neighbors layer by layer — like using highways to get close, then local streets for the exact destination.

      Follow-up: what do the M and ef_search parameters control, and which one affects index build versus query time?
      How does IVF differ from HNSW, and in what scenario would you actually prefer it?

      IVF clusters vectors into partitions via k-means and only searches the clusters nearest the query (n_probe of them), rather than traversing a graph. It generally has cheaper build/insert cost and lower memory than HNSW but a worse recall/speed tradeoff curve at moderate scale. It becomes attractive at very large (tens of millions+) scale, especially combined with product quantization (IVF-PQ) for memory-bounded billion-scale datasets, where HNSW's memory footprint would be prohibitive.

      Follow-up: why does IVF's recall degrade if the underlying data distribution shifts significantly after the clusters are built?
      How would you measure whether your ANN index's recall is actually good enough for your product, rather than assuming it?

      Take a representative sample of queries, run brute-force exact search on the same corpus to get ground-truth top-k results, run the same queries through the ANN index, and compute recall@k (fraction of the true top-k that the ANN index actually returned). Track this over time and after any index parameter or embedding model change, rather than treating default settings as sufficient.

      Follow-up: what tradeoff would you adjust (and in which direction) if measured recall is too low for your product's needs?
      Why can embedding a single, very long document as one vector hurt retrieval quality even though it's technically searchable?

      A single embedding has to represent the entire document's content in one fixed-length vector, averaging together potentially many distinct topics/sections — this dilutes the vector's ability to score highly against a query about any one specific topic within the document, since the vector is a blend, not a sharp representation of any single part. Chunking into smaller, topically coherent pieces, each separately embedded, produces vectors that can match specific queries much more precisely.

      Follow-up: what's a failure mode in the opposite direction — chunks that are too small?
      What operational challenge does HNSW have with deletions, and how is it typically handled?

      HNSW's graph structure doesn't support cheap, direct removal of a node without potentially breaking the navigability of connections through it. Common approaches: mark deleted vectors with a tombstone and filter them out of results without restructuring the graph (search cost creeps up over time as tombstones accumulate), or periodically rebuild the index from the current live data set.

      Follow-up: how would this affect your design for a vector DB backing content that changes or gets deleted frequently (e.g. a live chat history)?

      Advanced

      Design a zero-downtime migration from embedding model A to model B for a 10M-document production vector index.

      Key points: add a model/version field to vector metadata; dual-write new/updated documents under both A and B embeddings (or at minimum start fully embedding new content under B while A continues serving); run a backfill job re-embedding the historical corpus under B in batches, rate-limited against the embeddings API's cost/limits, tracking progress so it's resumable; once backfill completes, validate B's retrieval quality against a held-out eval set (compare to A's baseline quality, not just "it runs"); cut query traffic over to the B-only index behind a feature flag, with the ability to roll back to A if quality regresses; only then decommission A's vectors. Never serve a mixed A/B index to the same query.

      Follow-up: how would you validate retrieval quality objectively before cutting over, rather than relying on spot-checking?
      A production vector search feature returns plausible but subtly wrong results under load, and brute-force validation on the same queries returns different (better) results. Walk through your debugging process.

      First confirm it's a recall issue, not a data issue: verify the "better" brute-force results and the ANN results are searching the identical, consistently up-to-date dataset (rule out index lag/staleness first — a surprisingly common false lead). If data is confirmed consistent, measure recall@k systematically across a larger sample, not just the anecdotal failing cases, to see if this is pervasive or query-specific. If pervasive, inspect and likely increase ef_search (query-time recall/speed knob, cheap to tune without rebuilding) and separately M/ef_construction (build-time, requires reindex) based on where the recall/speed curve actually sits versus your latency budget. If query-specific, check whether those queries' true nearest neighbors are unusually close together in the vector space (a known harder case for ANN graphs) or whether metadata pre-filtering is interacting badly with the ANN search (filtering after retrieval can starve results if the filter is restrictive).

      Follow-up: why would increasing ef_search fix this without requiring an index rebuild, while increasing M would not?
      Explain product quantization (PQ) at a level sufficient to justify when its accuracy cost is worth it.

      PQ compresses each vector by splitting it into sub-vectors (segments of the original dimensions) and replacing each sub-vector with the index of its nearest centroid from a small, pre-trained codebook for that segment — storing a handful of small integer codes instead of the full floating-point vector, massively reducing memory (often 8-32x). Distance calculations become approximate (comparing codebook-centroid distances rather than true vector distances), costing additional recall on top of whatever ANN index wraps it (e.g. IVF-PQ). It's worth the cost specifically when memory is the binding constraint at very large scale (hundreds of millions to billions of vectors) where keeping full-precision vectors in memory/on fast storage is infeasible or prohibitively expensive, and the product's accuracy bar tolerates the additional loss.

      Follow-up: how would you validate that PQ's accuracy loss is acceptable for your specific product before committing to it at scale?
      How would you design metadata pre-filtering (e.g. multi-tenant isolation: "only search this tenant's documents") so it doesn't silently degrade recall or leak data across tenants?

      First, treat tenant isolation as a correctness/security requirement, not a recall optimization — verify explicitly whether your vector DB applies the filter during the ANN graph/cluster traversal (pre-filtering, generally better both for security guarantees and recall) versus after retrieving an unfiltered top-k and then filtering (post-filtering, which can return fewer than k results, or zero, if the tenant's documents are a small fraction of the corpus and didn't make the unfiltered top-k). For strict isolation guarantees independent of the ANN implementation's filtering quality, consider a per-tenant index/namespace instead of a single shared index with a filter, trading some operational complexity for a hard guarantee that cross-tenant leakage is structurally impossible rather than dependent on filter correctness.

      Follow-up: what's the operational cost of per-tenant indexes at scale (e.g. 100,000 tenants), and how would you mitigate it?
      Why might two cosine-similarity scores of 0.85 and 0.82 from two different embedding models NOT be directly comparable as "0.85 is more relevant than 0.82"?

      Different embedding models have different score distributions/calibration — one model's typical "good match" range might cluster around 0.7-0.9 while another's clusters around 0.4-0.6 for equivalent semantic relevance, due to differences in training objective, normalization, and the geometry the model learned. Raw similarity scores are only meaningfully comparable within the same model, same version, same normalization — never across models, and often not even perfectly comparable across very different query/document length distributions within the same model without calibration.

      Follow-up: how does this affect using a fixed similarity-score threshold (e.g. "only use chunks with score > 0.75") as a RAG relevance filter?

      Practice Assignments

      Beginner (30-60 min)

      Brute-force semantic search from scratch

      Given a small set of ~30 short text snippets, embed them with an embeddings API, store the vectors in memory, and implement brute-force cosine-similarity search to find the top-3 most relevant snippets for a free-text query.

      • No vector DB — plain arrays and a manual cosine similarity function.
      • Print the top-3 results with their scores for at least 5 different test queries.

      Acceptance criteria: results are subjectively sensible (a query about "refunds" returns refund-related snippets ahead of unrelated ones) for all 5 test queries.

      Hints

      Normalize vectors once after embedding if you want to use dot product as a cheaper stand-in for cosine similarity — verify both give the same ranking on a couple of examples first.

      Bonus: add one deliberately out-of-domain snippet and confirm it consistently scores low across all test queries.

      Intermediate (2-4 hrs)

      pgvector-backed search with HNSW and recall measurement

      Set up Postgres with the pgvector extension, embed a larger corpus (~2,000 short documents — e.g. Wikipedia summaries or your own notes), store them with an HNSW index, and measure the index's recall against brute-force ground truth.

      • Build the schema and HNSW index as shown in this chapter's code example.
      • For a sample of 20 queries, compute true top-10 via a brute-force SQL query (or in application code) and compare against the HNSW-indexed query's top-10, computing recall@10 for each.
      • Vary ef_search and report how recall and query latency change.

      Acceptance criteria: a table showing recall@10 and average query latency at least 3 different ef_search values, demonstrating the tradeoff explicitly.

      Hints

      pgvector lets you set ef_search per-session via SET hnsw.ef_search = N; before running a query, so you can sweep it without rebuilding the index.

      Bonus: repeat the experiment with an IVF-style approach (e.g. pgvector's own IVFFlat index type) and compare recall/latency tradeoffs against HNSW on the same data.

      Advanced (1-2 days)

      Embedding-model migration simulator

      Simulate the "upgrade embedding models without downtime" problem end to end on a corpus of your own documents, proving your migration strategy preserves or improves retrieval quality and never serves a mixed-model index to a single query.

      • Embed a corpus (~1,000 chunks) with model A; build a working search endpoint against it.
      • Build a labeled eval set (~20 queries with manually judged "good" results from the corpus).
      • Implement a migration path to model B: dual-track metadata, a resumable backfill job re-embedding the corpus under B, and a cutover mechanism (feature flag or versioned index) that only ever queries a single, consistent model's vectors per request.
      • Run your eval set against both A and B after migration and report quality (e.g. precision@5 against your labels) for each, plus confirm zero cross-model query/corpus mismatches occurred during the migration window.

      Acceptance criteria: a working before/after eval comparison, and a demonstrated (tested, not just argued) guarantee that no query during the migration ever compared vectors from different models.

      Hints

      The simplest safe cutover is two fully separate indexes/tables (one per model) with a single flag deciding which one is "live" for queries — resist the temptation to put both models' vectors in one table distinguished only by a metadata filter, since a filter bug there is exactly the failure mode you're trying to prevent.

      Bonus: add a canary step that routes 5% of query traffic to model B and compares its eval-set performance against the A baseline before a full cutover.

      Quick Revision Cheat Sheet

      • Embeddings encode meaning as direction in high-dimensional space; individual dimensions have no human-readable meaning.
      • Cosine similarity = angle only (ignores magnitude); use it for most text embedding models unless told otherwise.
      • Dot product ≡ cosine similarity only when vectors are normalized to unit length.
      • Exact (brute-force) search is fine under ~100k vectors; beyond that, reach for an ANN index.
      • HNSW: multi-layer graph, logarithmic-ish search, tunable via M (build) and ef_search (query) — default choice for most RAG workloads.
      • IVF/IVF-PQ: cluster-based, cheaper memory/build cost, worse recall curve than HNSW, used at very large scale.
      • Never mix embeddings from different models/versions in one comparison — re-embed the whole corpus on any model change.
      • Measure ANN recall@k against brute-force ground truth periodically — don't trust default parameters blindly.
      • Chunk before embedding — a single vector for a long document dilutes topical precision.
