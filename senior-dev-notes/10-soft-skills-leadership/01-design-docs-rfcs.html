    Writing Design Docs & RFCs

    ⏱ ~2 hrs📍 Chapter 1 of 7

    One-line summary: A good RFC gets a team to a decision in one review cycle; a bad one generates three weeks of Slack threads that end nowhere — the difference is almost entirely structure, not writing talent.

    On this page

      Why It Matters for a Senior Developer

      At senior level, you stop being judged only on code you write and start being judged on decisions you cause to happen correctly without you being in the room. A design doc is the mechanism for that. If you can't write one that a busy staff engineer can read in 10 minutes and either approve or push back on with a specific objection, you can't scale your influence past your own keyboard — you'll be stuck re-explaining the same context in every meeting, and decisions will drift to whoever talks the loudest in standup instead of whoever has the best reasoning.

      Why

      Writing forces you to notice the gaps in your own reasoning before a reviewer does. Most "I didn't think of that" moments in a design review are moments the author would have caught themselves if they'd been forced to write the alternative down instead of just gesturing at it verbally.

      Prerequisites

      No hard technical prerequisite. Related: Technical Decision Making (for picking between the alternatives you document) and Stakeholder Communication (for the executive-summary layer on top of a technical RFC).

      Core Concepts

      What an RFC is actually for

      An RFC (Request for Comments) or design doc is not documentation of a decision already made — it's the artifact that makes the decision happen efficiently. It exists to:

      • Force the author to commit to a specific proposal instead of hand-waving between options.
      • Let reviewers give feedback asynchronously, in parallel, instead of one meeting at a time.
      • Create a durable record of why, so six months later nobody re-litigates a decision from scratch because they can't remember the reasoning.
      • Surface disagreement early — on paper, before code is written — when it's cheap to change course.

      The single biggest failure mode is writing an RFC that only presents one option and asks "thoughts?" — that's not a design doc, that's an announcement. A real RFC names the alternatives you rejected and why, because the alternatives are where the real engineering judgment lives.

      The structure that works

      Length should match blast radius. A change affecting one team for two weeks might be half a page. A change affecting five teams and a data migration for six months might be four pages — rarely more. If you can't fit it in four pages, the scope is probably too large and should be split into phases with separate RFCs.

      Sections, in order, and why each one exists:

      • Context / Problem statement — what's broken or missing, with a concrete symptom (an incident, a metric, a blocked roadmap item), not "our architecture isn't scalable."
      • Goals and non-goals — non-goals are the most skipped and most valuable section. They stop reviewers from scope-creeping the discussion into a different problem.
      • Proposed solution — the one you recommend, described concretely enough that someone could start implementing it from this doc alone.
      • Alternatives considered — at least one or two, each with a one-line reason it was rejected. This is what proves you actually explored the space.
      • Tradeoffs / risks — what gets worse under your proposal, and what you'll do about it.
      • Rollout plan — phased, with a rollback path. "Big bang" rollouts are where senior reviewers push back hardest.
      • Open questions — things you genuinely don't know yet. Naming them builds trust; pretending to have no open questions destroys it.

      Running the review process, not just writing the doc

      The doc is half the job; getting it reviewed and closed out is the other half.

      • Pick reviewers deliberately. One domain expert who can poke real holes, one person who'll be most affected operationally, one generalist senior/staff engineer for outside perspective. Reviewing by "whoever happens to see the Slack link" produces noise, not signal.
      • Set a deadline for comments and say what happens if nobody comments by then (you proceed). Open-ended review cycles never close.
      • Triage comments by type: blocking ("this will break X"), strong opinion ("I'd do Y instead but won't block"), nit (naming, wording). Resolve blocking comments explicitly in the doc, don't just reply in the thread — the next reader of the doc should see the resolution.
      • Call a synchronous meeting only when text has stalled — i.e., the same point has gone back and forth twice in comments with no convergence. Don't default to a meeting; it kills the efficiency gain of writing async in the first place.
      • Record the decision at the top of the doc once it's made: status (Draft / In Review / Approved / Superseded), date, and who approved it. Future readers should not have to read the whole comment thread to find out if this is even still the plan.

      How It Works in Practice

      A realistic timeline for a medium-sized RFC (affects 2-3 teams): draft over 1-2 days, share with the 3 chosen reviewers with a 3-business-day comment deadline, triage and resolve comments over the next 1-2 days, final read-through, mark Approved. Total: about a week from first draft to decision. If it's taking three weeks, usually one of: the doc didn't name a clear recommendation (too wishy-washy to agree or disagree with), the wrong reviewers were picked (too many, or missing the one domain expert whose objection everyone is waiting to hear), or disagreement went into a side Slack DM instead of being resolved in the doc where the deadline pressure and public visibility actually work.

      Templates / Example Scripts

      # RFC: [Short, specific title — not "Improve Payments"]

**Status:** Draft | In Review | Approved | Superseded
**Author:** Jane Doe   **Date:** 2026-10-03   **Reviewers:** @alex @priya @sam

## Context / Problem
Checkout latency p95 is 2.1s, SLA is 800ms. Root cause: payment-service calls
three downstream providers synchronously per request. This blocks the
Q4 "1-click checkout" roadmap item.

## Goals
- Get checkout p95 under 800ms without changing the provider contract.
- No increase in failed-payment rate.

## Non-goals
- Not re-architecting the provider integrations themselves.
- Not solving payment-service's unrelated memory leak (tracked in INFRA-412).

## Proposed Solution
Run the three provider calls concurrently with a 600ms timeout each and a
circuit breaker per provider. Cache provider "health" status in Redis
(30s TTL) to skip known-down providers immediately instead of waiting
for their timeout.

[architecture diagram / sequence diagram here]

## Alternatives Considered
1. **Async webhook-based confirmation** — rejected: requires provider
   contract changes we can't get agreed before Q4.
2. **Pre-warm all three providers on cart creation** — rejected: wastes
   ~40% of calls (cart abandonment rate is 40%), raises our provider cost.

## Tradeoffs / Risks
- Concurrent calls raise peak outbound connection count ~3x. Mitigation:
  connection pool sized for 3x current peak, load-tested before rollout.
- Circuit breaker false-positives could skip a provider that's actually fine
  during a network blip. Mitigation: 3-failure threshold, 30s half-open probe.

## Rollout Plan
1. Ship behind a feature flag, 1% of traffic, 48h bake.
2. Ramp 1% -> 25% -> 100% over 5 business days, watch p95 + failure rate.
3. Rollback: flip the flag off; old synchronous path stays in code for one
   release cycle before removal.

## Open Questions
- Does provider C's rate limit allow 3x concurrent call volume at peak?
  (Checking with their support, ETA answer by Fri.)

      # Bad: vague, unreviewable
We should probably make the payment calls faster, maybe parallel or

# Good: concrete, falsifiable, reviewable
Run the 3 provider calls concurrently with a 600ms per-call timeout and
a circuit breaker per provider (3-failure threshold, 30s half-open).
Expected p95: ~650ms (longest of 3 parallel calls vs sum of 3 sequential).

      Real-World Scenario at Scale (large team / org)

      At scale

      At orgs with dozens of teams, RFCs that touch shared infrastructure (a core API, a shared database, a platform library) need an explicit "affected teams" list and a sign-off checkbox per team lead, not just open comments — otherwise a team finds out about a breaking change the week it ships, not the week it was proposed. Many large orgs also run a lightweight RFC index/registry (a wiki page or doc folder with status + owner) so a new hire can find "what's the current plan for X" without archaeology through Slack history. The single biggest org-scale failure is RFC sprawl: dozens of "Approved" docs that were never actually implemented and nobody marked Superseded — treat an RFC's status field as a long-term promise to keep current, not a one-time label.

      Common Mistakes, Gotchas and Best Practices

      Gotcha

      Writing the "Alternatives Considered" section after the fact, to justify a decision you'd already made informally, instead of genuinely before. Reviewers can tell — the rejected alternatives read as strawmen with no real tradeoff. Write this section honestly, including an alternative that's actually tempting, and give the real reason you didn't pick it.

      • Mistake: no clear recommendation — presenting 3 options as equally weighted and asking the room to pick. That's abdicating the judgment call you were asked to make. Recommend one, and let reviewers argue you out of it if they disagree.
      • Mistake: burying the problem statement under implementation detail. A reviewer should understand why this matters in the first 30 seconds.
      • Best practice: write the one-paragraph summary/TL;DR at the very top, even though you'll write it last — busy senior reviewers often only read that paragraph plus the alternatives section.
      • Best practice: timebox the writing itself. An RFC is a tool to get to a decision, not a thesis. If you're on draft 5 and still polishing prose, you're avoiding sending it for feedback.

      Interview Questions

      Basic

      What is the purpose of a design doc / RFC?

      To force a concrete proposal (not just vague discussion), surface alternatives and tradeoffs, let reviewers give async feedback in parallel, and create a durable record of why a decision was made. It's the mechanism that gets a team to a decision efficiently and records the reasoning for later.

      Follow-up: why not just discuss it in a meeting?
      What sections should every design doc have at minimum?

      Context/problem, goals and non-goals, proposed solution, alternatives considered, risks/tradeoffs, and a rollout plan. Non-goals and alternatives are the two most commonly skipped but most valuable.

      Follow-up: why are non-goals valuable?
      Why should an RFC list alternatives it rejected?

      It proves the author actually explored the solution space rather than anchoring on the first idea, and gives reviewers a concrete thing to disagree with if they think a rejected option was actually better.

      Follow-up: what makes a rejected-alternative section weak?
      How long should a design doc be?

      Proportional to blast radius — half a page for a small single-team change, up to ~4 pages for something affecting several teams over months. If it's longer than that, the scope should probably be split into phases.

      Follow-up: what do you do when a proposal doesn't fit in 4 pages?
      Who should review an RFC?

      A small, deliberately chosen set: a domain expert who can find real holes, someone most operationally affected, and a generalist senior/staff engineer for outside perspective — not "whoever sees the link in Slack."

      Follow-up: what goes wrong if you pick too many reviewers?
      What does "status: Approved" on a doc mean, and why record it?

      It's a durable marker of the decision and date, so future readers don't have to read the whole comment thread to know if the plan is current. It should be updated to "Superseded" when a later decision replaces it.

      Follow-up: what's RFC sprawl and why is it a problem?
      When is it OK to skip writing a design doc entirely?

      When the change is trivial, fully and cheaply reversible, and single-owner — nothing anyone would reasonably ask "why did we do it this way" about six months later.

      Follow-up: give an example from your own recent work.

      Medium

      Your RFC has been in review for three weeks with no resolution. What do you do?

      Diagnose why: no clear recommendation (too wishy-washy to agree/disagree with), wrong reviewer set, or disagreement happening in side DMs instead of in the doc. Fix the specific cause — add a clear recommendation, re-scope reviewers, pull disagreement back into the doc with a hard comment deadline — rather than just pinging "any updates?" again.

      Follow-up: when do you escalate to a sync meeting?
      A reviewer leaves a strong objection to your recommended approach after everyone else has already approved. How do you handle it?

      Take it seriously regardless of timing — re-open the relevant section, ask for their specific concern in writing, and evaluate on merit. If it's a real blocking issue, revise the doc and re-notify all reviewers; don't just overrule it because "everyone else already said yes." If it's a preference not a blocker, note it as a considered-and-declined alternative.

      Follow-up: how do you avoid this happening repeatedly from the same reviewer?
      How do you write a design doc for a problem you don't yet have a strong recommendation for?

      Still write it with a recommendation — pick the option you lean toward with your current information, flag your confidence level explicitly ("60% confident"), and put your real uncertainty in Open Questions with a plan to resolve it (a spike, a conversation with a specific team) rather than leaving the whole doc open-ended.

      Follow-up: what's wrong with presenting 3 options with no recommendation at all?
      How do design docs differ for a reversible vs an irreversible decision?

      Reversible decisions (a config value, a feature flag default) need a lighter doc and faster review since the cost of being wrong is low. Irreversible ones (a public API contract, a data model that's hard to migrate, a vendor lock-in) need a fuller alternatives/risk section and more deliberate reviewer sign-off, because reversing course later is expensive.

      Follow-up: how does this map to Amazon's one-way vs two-way door framing?
      How do you write the rollout plan section for a change with a database migration involved?

      Phase it: dual-write or backward-compatible schema change first, backfill, verify data consistency with a read-path comparison, then cut over reads, then remove the old path — each phase independently deployable and reversible. State the rollback path for each phase explicitly, not just "we'll roll back if needed."

      Follow-up: why phase it instead of a single migration deploy?
      How do you keep a design doc from becoming stale after it's approved?

      Treat the status field as a living promise: update to Superseded when a later decision replaces it, link forward/backward between related docs, and periodically (e.g., during a related incident or retro) check whether "Approved" docs still reflect reality.

      Follow-up: whose job is it to keep docs current — the author's, forever?

      Advanced

      You're asked to write an RFC for a decision your manager has effectively already made informally. How do you handle the "Alternatives Considered" section honestly?

      Write it as a genuine exploration anyway — list the alternative that's actually tempting (not a strawman) and give the real reason it was deprioritized. If you can't find a real reason beyond "leadership preference," say that explicitly rather than fabricating a technical justification; a fabricated justification will be spotted by any sharp reviewer and damages your credibility more than an honest "this was already directionally decided, here's the reasoning" would.

      Follow-up: what if a reviewer then raises an objection to the "already decided" approach — what's your move?
      How would you redesign your org's RFC process if docs were being approved but routinely not implemented as written?

      Add a lightweight implementation-tracking step: link the RFC to the tracking ticket/epic, and require an "implementation diverged from doc" note (plus a doc update) if the shipped version differs materially. Also check whether approval is happening too easily/early — sometimes divergence during implementation reveals the doc wasn't detailed enough to be followed as written, which is itself a sign to require more concreteness before Approved status.

      Follow-up: how do you avoid turning this into bureaucratic overhead that slows teams down?
      Describe a time you had to write an RFC for a decision you personally disagreed with. How did you approach it?

      (Scenario-based — strong answers describe presenting the recommended option fairly and completely even while personally preferring an alternative, documenting that alternative honestly in the Alternatives section with its real tradeoffs, and separating "what I'd personally choose" from "what the doc recommends based on the team's stated priorities" rather than silently sandbagging the option they disagree with.)

      Follow-up: where's the line between advocating for your view and being a team player once a decision is made?
      How do you scope an RFC review process differently for a proposal that changes a public API contract used by external customers versus an internal-only service?

      External-facing changes need a much higher bar: explicit versioning/deprecation plan, a defined support window for the old contract, and often legal/support/docs team sign-off in addition to engineering — because the "blast radius" includes parties you can't coordinate a synchronized rollout with. Internal-only changes can move faster since all consumers are known and can be migrated in lockstep.

      Follow-up: how would you handle a customer who's already built on a contract you're about to deprecate?
      Your RFC proposes a technically superior solution that would take 3x longer than a "good enough" alternative, and leadership is pushing for speed. How do you write the doc?

      Present both honestly with real numbers (cost of each, cost of technical debt incurred by the faster path, and a concrete plan/timeline for paying that debt down later), and let leadership make the business call explicitly and visibly in the doc rather than making it for them by only presenting the option you prefer. Record whichever is chosen along with the debt tradeoff accepted, so it's not relitigated later as if nobody saw it coming.

      Follow-up: six months later the debt from the fast path caused an outage — how do you reference this RFC in the retro without it reading as "I told you so"?

      Practice Assignments

      Beginner (30-60 min)

      Rewrite a vague proposal

      Take this real-world-style vague proposal: "We should move our logging to a better system, the current one is kind of a mess and hard to search." Rewrite it as a proper one-page RFC using the template from this chapter.

      • Include a concrete problem statement (invent realistic symptoms: search latency, missing fields, cost).
      • Include at least 2 alternatives with real rejection reasons.
      • Include a rollout plan with a rollback path.

      Acceptance criteria: A reviewer with no other context could approve or raise one specific objection after reading it in under 5 minutes.

      Hints

      Pick one specific logging system as your recommendation (e.g., ELK vs a managed service) and commit to it — don't leave it open.

      Bonus: Write the "Open Questions" section with at least one question you genuinely couldn't answer without more research.

      Intermediate (2-4 hrs)

      Write a real RFC for your own work

      Pick a real technical decision you've made (or are currently facing) at work or in a side project — a library choice, a data model change, a service split. Write the full RFC.

      • Must include a real, specific problem statement with a symptom or metric.
      • Must include at least one alternative you were genuinely tempted by.
      • Must include a phased rollout plan with rollback.

      Acceptance criteria: Share it with one real colleague or mentor and get at least one piece of substantive feedback (not just "looks good").

      Hints

      If you can't find a real alternative you were tempted by, you probably haven't explored the space enough yet — that's a sign to do 30 more minutes of research before writing the doc, not to fabricate one.

      Bonus: Have someone play a skeptical reviewer and give you one hard pushback question; write your response.

      Advanced (1-2 days)

      Design an RFC process for a 40-person engineering org

      Your company has grown from 8 engineers (where decisions happened by walking over to someone's desk) to 40 across 6 teams, and decisions are now getting made inconsistently — some teams write great docs, others ship breaking changes with zero notice. Design the RFC process: templates, review SLAs, who approves what tier of change, and how you avoid both "nothing ships because of process" and "nothing is documented."

      • Define at least 2 tiers of change (e.g., team-local vs cross-team) with different review requirements.
      • Define what happens when a team ignores the process.
      • Define how you'll know in 3 months whether the process is working or just adding overhead.
      Hints (no full solution)

      Think about what signal you'd actually measure — review cycle time, number of post-ship surprises/incidents tied to undocumented changes — rather than just "process compliance."

      Quick Revision Cheat Sheet

      • RFC = decision-making tool, not after-the-fact documentation.
      • Required sections: context, goals/non-goals, proposal, alternatives, risks, rollout.
      • Length scales with blast radius — half a page to ~4 pages max.
      • Always name a clear recommendation, not just a menu of equal options.
      • Pick 2-3 deliberate reviewers, not "whoever sees the Slack link."
      • Set a comment deadline; default to proceeding if nobody objects by then.
      • Resolve blocking comments in the doc itself, not just in thread replies.
      • Record status (Draft/In Review/Approved/Superseded) + date at the top.
      • Reversible decisions get lighter docs; irreversible ones get fuller risk sections.
      • Keep approved docs current — mark Superseded, don't let RFC sprawl accumulate.
