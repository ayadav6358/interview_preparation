    Technical Decision Making & Handling Disagreement

    ⏱ ~2.5 hrs📍 Chapter 6 of 7

    One-line summary: Senior engineers are judged less on whether they're always right and more on whether they make decisions with a clear, defensible process, disagree productively without making it personal, and commit fully once a call is made even if it wasn't their preferred option.

    On this page

      Why It Matters for a Senior Developer

      Junior engineers mostly execute decisions someone else made. Senior engineers are expected to make real architectural and technical calls under incomplete information, defend them with reasoning rather than authority, and — just as important — handle it well when a decision goes against their own preference. Teams that can't disagree productively either avoid hard conversations (leading to silent resentment and worse decisions) or turn disagreements into status fights (leading to burned relationships and decisions made by whoever is loudest, not whoever is right).

      Why

      A decision made with a documented, falsifiable rationale can be revisited cheaply when new information arrives — "here's what we knew and why we chose X" is easy to update. A decision made by authority or gut feel with no stated reasoning is expensive to revisit because there's nothing to argue against except the person themselves.

      Prerequisites

      No hard technical prerequisite. Related: Writing Design Docs & RFCs (the artifact that usually carries a decision's rationale) and Stakeholder Communication (for communicating a decision outward once it's made).

      Core Concepts

      Reversible vs irreversible decisions

      Not all decisions deserve the same process. A reversible decision (easy and cheap to undo — a library choice behind a clean interface, a naming convention) should be made quickly by whoever's closest to the work, without a long debate. An irreversible or expensive-to-reverse decision (a database choice with a hard migration path, a public API contract, a core architectural pattern the whole team will build on) deserves slower, more deliberate process — more input gathered, more explicit tradeoff analysis, often a written doc. Spending a week debating a reversible decision and five minutes on an irreversible one is backwards, and it's a common failure mode on teams that haven't made this distinction explicit.

      Disagree and commit, and the DACI pattern

      "Disagree and commit" means: voice your disagreement fully and honestly during the decision process, but once the decision is made by whoever has the authority to make it, commit to executing it well — not undermining it, not quietly doing it your way, not re-litigating it in every subsequent meeting. This only works if disagreement was genuinely heard before the decision, not just tolerated as noise.

      DACI — a lightweight framework for who decides what

Driver    — runs the decision process, gathers input, keeps it moving
Approver  — the person who actually makes the final call (often one person)
Contributors — people whose input is sought and genuinely considered
Informed  — people told the outcome, not consulted beforehand

Example — choosing a message queue for a new service:
  Driver: the engineer proposing the change, writes the RFC
  Approver: the tech lead / staff engineer accountable for the area
  Contributors: 2-3 engineers who'll build on it, plus SRE for ops concerns
  Informed: the rest of the team, via the merged RFC and a short summary

Naming these roles explicitly up front avoids the common failure where
everyone assumes they're a Contributor-or-above and the decision never
actually closes.

      Handling disagreement with a manager or someone more senior

      Disagreeing with someone who has more authority or experience than you requires more care but is often more valuable precisely because they have blind spots their position creates. The approach that works: lead with genuine curiosity about their reasoning before asserting your own ("help me understand why X, because I was leaning toward Y for reason Z — what am I missing?") rather than opening with a flat counter-claim. This does two things — it surfaces information you might be missing (maybe they know a constraint you don't), and if you're actually right, it gives them room to update without feeling publicly contradicted, which makes it far more likely they actually will.

      How It Works in Practice

      A workable decision process for a non-trivial technical call: name the decision and its reversibility explicitly, identify who the Approver actually is, gather input from real Contributors (including anyone who'll be most affected by being wrong), write down the real options considered and the tradeoff that drove the choice (even two sentences beats nothing), make the call, and communicate it with the reasoning — not just the conclusion — to everyone Informed. Then, critically: once made, defend it as a team decision in front of outsiders even if you personally argued against it, and reserve re-opening it for genuinely new information, not just lingering personal preference.

Options considered:
  1. [option] - [key pro] / [key con]
  2. [option] - [key pro] / [key con]

Driver / Approver / Contributors / Informed: [names or roles]
Revisit if: [the specific new information that would reopen this]

# Bad vs good: disagreeing with a more senior person

## Bad: opens with a flat counter-claim, no curiosity
"I don't think that's right. We should use Y instead."

## Good: curiosity first, reasoning stated, room for them to update
"Help me understand the reasoning for X - I was leaning toward Y
because of [specific reason]. Is there a constraint I'm missing,
or does Y's tradeoff actually work here too?"

---

# Bad vs good: committing after a decision you disagreed with

## Bad: quietly undermines it afterward
"Well, I thought we should've done Y, but whatever, we're doing X I guess."
[said in front of the rest of the team, repeatedly]

## Good: argued hard beforehand, now fully committed
"We went with X after weighing it against Y - here's the reasoning
[the real tradeoff, not just 'leadership decided']. I'll flag if I
see something that changes the picture, but right now we're building
on X."

      Real-World Scenario at Scale (large team / org)

      At scale

      In a larger org, the same architectural decision often gets re-litigated by different people encountering it for the first time, months apart, because the original reasoning was never written down anywhere discoverable. The fix is a lightweight, searchable decision log (even a simple ADR — Architecture Decision Record — directory in the repo) that any engineer can check before re-opening a debate someone already had. This doesn't prevent legitimate re-opening when real new information exists; it prevents the much more common and wasteful case of relitigating from scratch because nobody could find out a decision, and its reasoning, had already been made.

      Common Mistakes, Gotchas and Best Practices

      Gotcha

      Treating every technical disagreement as something that must be "won." Most technical disagreements are actually about different weightings of a real tradeoff (e.g., "I value simplicity more, you value flexibility more") rather than one side being factually wrong — once framed that way, the conversation becomes "what does this specific situation need more of" instead of a battle over who's right in general.

      Gotcha

      Agreeing to "disagree and commit" in the room but then slow-rolling or quietly implementing your preferred alternative anyway. This is more corrosive to trust than openly re-raising the disagreement through legitimate channels, because it's covert — teammates sense the undermining even when they can't name it.

      • Mistake: spending disproportionate process (meetings, docs, debate) on a cheaply reversible decision while rushing an expensive, hard-to-undo one.
      • Mistake: making a unilateral call on something that affects multiple teams without naming who the real Contributors should have been, discovered only after the decision breaks something for a team that was never consulted.
      • Best practice: write down the options actually considered, not just the conclusion — the record of "we also considered X and rejected it because Y" is often more valuable later than the decision itself.
      • Best practice: explicitly name what new information would cause you to revisit a decision at the time you make it — this prevents both premature relitigating and stubborn refusal to update later.

      Interview Questions

      Basic

      What's the difference between how you should approach a reversible vs an irreversible technical decision?

      A reversible, cheap-to-undo decision should be made quickly by whoever's closest to the work. An expensive-to-reverse decision deserves slower, more deliberate process — more input, explicit tradeoff analysis, usually a written record.

      Follow-up: give an example of each from your own recent work.
      What does "disagree and commit" mean, and what has to be true for it to work fairly?

      Voice disagreement fully before a decision is made, then commit to executing it well once it's made, even if it wasn't your preference. It only works fairly if the disagreement was genuinely heard and considered first — not just tolerated as noise before a predetermined outcome.

      Follow-up: what do you do if you commit but then new information arrives that changes the picture?
      What are the four roles in a DACI decision framework?

      Driver (runs the process), Approver (makes the final call), Contributors (input genuinely sought and considered), Informed (told the outcome afterward, not consulted beforehand).

      Follow-up: what commonly goes wrong when these roles aren't named explicitly?
      Why should you write down the options you considered and rejected, not just the final decision?

      The rejected-options reasoning is often more valuable later than the decision itself — it tells a future reader whether a new idea was already considered and why it didn't work, preventing wasted relitigation.

      Follow-up: how much detail is actually worth writing down for a routine decision?
      Why is "I don't think that's right, we should do Y instead" a weaker opening than leading with curiosity when disagreeing with someone more senior?

      A flat counter-claim gives the other person nothing to work with except defend their position, and skips the chance that they know a constraint you don't. Leading with curiosity about their reasoning surfaces missing information on both sides and makes it easier for either party to update without feeling publicly contradicted.

      Follow-up: what if their reasoning turns out to be weak once explained?
      What's a common failure mode when a team tries to reach full consensus on every decision?

      Consensus is often unreachable in practice or takes disproportionate time, especially under deadline pressure, and can let the loudest or most stubborn person functionally veto a reasonable decision. A named Approver who genuinely weighs input is usually more workable.

      Follow-up: when, if ever, is true consensus actually worth pursuing?

      Medium

      How do you tell whether a technical disagreement is actually about facts versus about differing tradeoff priorities?

      Ask what each side is optimizing for (e.g., simplicity vs. flexibility, speed vs. correctness) — if both sides agree on the facts but weight the priorities differently, it's a tradeoff disagreement, which is resolved by asking what this specific situation needs more of, not by proving one side "wrong." If the facts themselves are disputed, that's resolvable with evidence instead.

      Follow-up: what if you genuinely can't tell which type of disagreement it is?
      A decision you disagreed with turns out, a month later, to have been a mistake. How do you raise it?

      Raise it based on the new evidence now available, not as a vindication of your original position — frame it as "here's what we're seeing now that we didn't know when we decided" rather than "I told you so." Focus the conversation on what to do next, not on relitigating who was right.

      Follow-up: how do you avoid this coming across as told-you-so even if you genuinely did warn about this exact risk?
      You're the Approver on a decision and two Contributors give you directly conflicting strong recommendations. How do you proceed?

      Dig into why they disagree — often it's a hidden difference in assumptions or priorities rather than one being simply wrong. Make the call explicitly once you understand the real tradeoff, document the reasoning so both contributors can see their input was genuinely weighed even if not fully adopted, and don't let the deadlock stall the decision indefinitely.

      Follow-up: what if one of the contributors reacts badly to not being followed?
      How do you handle disagree-and-commit when you believe the decision is not just suboptimal but actually risky?

      Distinguish "I'd have chosen differently" from "this creates a real, specific risk that needs to be flagged" — the latter deserves explicit escalation of the risk itself (not just the preference) before committing, ideally with a concrete ask like a smaller pilot or a defined checkpoint to catch the risk early, rather than either silent compliance or refusal to execute.

      Follow-up: what if you escalate the risk and leadership proceeds anyway?
      How do you decide who the real "Contributors" should be for a cross-team technical decision, avoiding both too few and too many?

      Include people who will be meaningfully affected by being wrong (they'll build on it, operate it, or be blocked by it) and people with relevant expertise the Approver lacks — not everyone who's merely interested. Too few risks a blind spot breaking something later; too many turns input-gathering into a slow consensus process that never closes.

      Follow-up: how do you handle someone who insists on being a Contributor but doesn't have clearly relevant stake or expertise?

      Advanced

      Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager on a technical decision. How did you handle it, and what was the outcome?

      (Scenario-based — strong answers lead with curiosity about the manager's reasoning before asserting a counter-position, state their own reasoning clearly and specifically rather than vaguely, and describe either genuinely updating their view on learning new context, or committing fully to the manager's call after being heard, without lingering resentment or covert undermining.)

      Follow-up: how would you have handled it differently if the manager had simply overruled you without hearing you out?
      Describe a technical decision you made that, in hindsight, was wrong. How did you discover it, and what did you do?

      (Scenario-based — strong answers name the specific decision and the real tradeoff at the time (why it looked right given what was known), how the mistake surfaced (ideally through monitoring or feedback rather than catastrophic failure), and concrete corrective action taken plus what changed in their decision process afterward, not just "we fixed it.")

      Follow-up: what would have had to be true at decision time for you to have avoided this, realistically?
      How would you redesign a team's decision-making process if technical decisions are consistently relitigated for months after being made?

      Diagnose whether the root cause is undocumented reasoning (fix: lightweight decision records, discoverable by anyone before reopening a debate), unclear ownership (fix: explicit DACI roles so it's clear whose call it was and why), or genuinely unresolved disagreement that was never actually heard before the decision closed (fix: a real input-gathering step before the Approver decides, not after).

      Follow-up: how do you distinguish legitimate reopening due to new information from unproductive relitigating?
      You're the most senior engineer in a room and strongly believe the group consensus is wrong on an architectural call. How do you handle it?

      Your seniority gives you more credibility to be heard, which raises the responsibility to use it carefully — state your reasoning clearly and specifically rather than relying on authority ("because I said so" or implicit deference), invite direct challenge to your own reasoning, and be genuinely willing to be wrong if the group has information you don't. If you still disagree after a real exchange, name that explicitly as a flagged risk even if you ultimately defer to whoever the actual Approver is.

      Follow-up: what if your seniority causes people to defer to you even when you explicitly invite challenge?
      How do you build a culture where disagree-and-commit is trusted, rather than becoming a euphemism for "junior people's input doesn't actually matter"?

      The test is whether dissenting input visibly changes decisions at least sometimes — if the Approver's documented reasoning never reflects points raised by Contributors, trust in the process erodes regardless of what's said about it. Make a habit of explicitly naming which part of the final call came from a specific person's pushback, even when the overall decision doesn't fully change.

      Follow-up: how do you measure whether this culture is actually working versus just looking like it is?

      Practice Assignments

      Beginner (30-60 min)

      Classify 5 real decisions by reversibility

      List 5 technical decisions from your current project (recent or upcoming). For each, classify as cheaply reversible, expensive to reverse, or effectively permanent, and state the process it actually deserves (fast individual call vs. documented DACI process).

      • At least one decision should be one your team is currently over- or under-investing process in.

      Acceptance criteria: For each decision, you can name the specific cost of getting the reversibility classification wrong in either direction.

      Hints

      If you're unsure how reversible something is, ask "what would it actually take to undo this in 3 months" — the honest answer to that question is usually the real classifier.

      Bonus: Write a two-sentence lightweight decision record for the one you're currently under-investing process in.

      Intermediate (2-4 hrs)

      Write a disagree-and-commit message for a realistic scenario

      Scenario: your tech lead decided to use a third-party library you believe is poorly maintained and risky for a core piece of infrastructure, after hearing your concern. Write the message you'd send to your team committing to the decision, plus the private note to yourself naming exactly what new evidence would make you raise it again.

      • The team-facing message must not undermine the decision, even subtly.
      • Must name the real tradeoff considered, not just "leadership decided."
      • The private note must name concrete, observable evidence, not vague unease.

      Acceptance criteria: A teammate reading only the team-facing message would have no idea you disagreed with the choice.

      Hints

      If your "concrete evidence" is something like "if it feels risky," it's not concrete yet — make it something checkable, like "if the library misses two consecutive release cycles" or "if we hit the specific bug class we were worried about."

      Bonus: Write the message you'd send 3 months later if that evidence actually materialized.

      Advanced (1-2 days)

      Design a decision-record system for a team that keeps relitigating old calls

      Hypothetical: a 20-person engineering org keeps re-debating the same handful of architectural decisions every few months because nobody remembers (or can find) the original reasoning. Design a lightweight system to fix this.

      • Specify the concrete artifact format (not just "write docs") and where it lives so it's actually discoverable before a debate starts, not after.
      • Address how you'd retroactively capture reasoning for past decisions that were never documented.
      • Propose how you'd keep the system from becoming unused busywork after the initial enthusiasm fades.
      Hints (no full solution)

      Consider that the system fails if writing a record is more effort than just re-deciding from scratch — the format needs to be fast enough that it's actually the path of least resistance, and tied into an existing workflow (like PR/RFC review) rather than a separate step people forget.

      Quick Revision Cheat Sheet

      • Match process weight to reversibility — fast calls for cheap-to-undo, deliberate process for expensive-to-undo.
      • DACI: Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed — name the roles explicitly.
      • Disagree and commit only works if disagreement was genuinely heard first.
      • Lead disagreement with someone senior with curiosity about their reasoning, not a flat counter-claim.
      • Most technical disagreements are tradeoff-priority differences, not fact disputes.
      • Document options considered and rejected, not just the final decision.
      • Name what new information would reopen a decision, at decision time.
      • Never covertly undermine a decision you agreed to commit to.
      • Full consensus is rarely necessary or practical — a genuinely-considered Approver call usually beats it.
