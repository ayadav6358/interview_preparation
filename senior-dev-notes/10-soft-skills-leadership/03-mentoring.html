    Mentoring Juniors

    ⏱ ~2 hrs📍 Chapter 3 of 7

    One-line summary: Good mentoring makes someone capable of solving the next ten problems without you, not just this one — if you're still doing their thinking for them after three months, you're not mentoring, you're a bottleneck with extra steps.

    On this page

      Why It Matters for a Senior Developer

      Every senior/staff-level job description includes "mentors junior engineers" because it's one of the few ways an individual contributor multiplies their impact beyond their own output. Interviewers test for it because a team of ten with one strong senior who never grows anyone else caps out; a team where the senior grows three more mid-levels into strong contributors compounds. It's also one of the clearest differentiators between "senior by title" and "senior by behavior" — anyone can write good code alone in a dark room; growing someone else requires patience, self-awareness, and a different kind of skill.

      Why

      If you answer every question by just giving the answer, you optimize for this week's velocity and ensure the same question (or a harder version of it) lands on your desk again next month. Teaching someone to find the answer costs more time once and less time forever after.

      Prerequisites

      No hard technical prerequisite. Related: Code Reviews That Improve Teams (review is often the entry point into a mentoring relationship) and Technical Decision Making (teaching someone to make calls, not just execute yours).

      Core Concepts

      Levels of help, from least to most hand-holding

      When someone brings you a problem, resist jumping straight to the answer. A useful ladder, cheapest and most growth-building at the top:

      • Ask a clarifying question that gets them to notice the gap themselves ("What have you tried so far? What did you expect to happen vs what happened?").
      • Point at where to look without giving the answer ("Check what the logs say around the timestamp of the failure").
      • Give a hint toward the mechanism ("This smells like a race condition — what runs concurrently here?").
      • Pair on it together, narrating your own thought process out loud so they see how you reason, not just the result.
      • Just give the answer — reserved for genuine emergencies (production is down) or when the thing isn't actually a learning opportunity (an obscure tool quirk with no transferable lesson).

      The skill is picking the right rung for the situation and the person's current level, not always defaulting to the bottom (fastest for you, worst for them) or the top (slowest, sometimes patronizing if they already have the skill and just need a second set of eyes).

      Structuring a mentoring relationship

      Ad-hoc "let me know if you're stuck" mentoring is better than nothing but leaves growth to chance. A structured relationship has:

      • A named growth area, agreed together — not just "be a better engineer" but something concrete: "get comfortable debugging production incidents without panicking," "learn to break a large feature into independently shippable PRs."
      • Regular, protected time — a recurring 30-minute 1:1, even if some weeks it's "nothing specific, how's it going," because the regularity itself signals they matter and gives a natural checkpoint.
      • Stretch assignments — deliberately give them something slightly past their current comfort zone, with you as a safety net, rather than only giving them well-trodden tickets.
      • Explicit, timely feedback — both positive and corrective, close to the event, not saved up for a quarterly review where it's stale and disconnected from the moment.

      Handling the hard cases

      Two situations trip up even experienced mentors:

      • The mentee who's plateaued. Someone solid at their current level who isn't growing. Often the cause is comfort (no stretch assignments lately) or a skills gap they're quietly avoiding (e.g., avoiding anything involving public speaking or incident command). Name it directly and specifically rather than vaguely hoping they'll self-diagnose.
      • The mentee who's becoming a peer or surpassing you in some dimension. This is success, not a threat — but it requires you to shift from "teach" to "collaborate as equals in this area," and some mentors unconsciously resist that shift. If you notice yourself nitpicking someone's work more as they get better, check whether that's really about their work or about your own discomfort.
      • Mentoring someone outside your reporting line or team. You have influence but no formal authority — feedback has to be even more clearly framed as "take it or leave it, your call" since you can't back it with a performance review.

      How It Works in Practice

      A realistic cadence: weekly or biweekly 30-minute 1:1, a running shared doc of topics/questions between sessions (so nothing gets lost to memory), explicit mid-point check-ins on any stretch assignment ("how's the migration going, where are you stuck") rather than waiting for them to ask, and a periodic (monthly or quarterly) step back to ask "what's the growth area for the next period" rather than letting the 1:1 drift into pure status updates. The highest-value single habit: after any time you helped someone solve a problem, ask yourself "could they solve the next similar one alone now?" — if the honest answer is no, you gave an answer instead of teaching a method, and that's worth correcting next time.

      Templates / Example Scripts

      # Example mentoring dialogue: bad vs good

## Bad: just gives the answer, no learning transferred
Mentee: "My test is failing and I don't know why."
Mentor: "Oh, it's because you're not awaiting the promise on line 12.
         Add `await` there."
Mentee: "Oh ok, thanks!" [learns nothing about HOW to find this next time]

## Good: ladder of questions that builds a debugging habit
Mentee: "My test is failing and I don't know why."
Mentor: "What's the actual error message say?"
Mentee: "Something about 'Cannot read property of undefined'."
Mentor: "OK — where in the stack trace does it point?"
Mentee: "Line 12, the `user.name` access."
Mentor: "So `user` is undefined there. Where does `user` come from on
         that line?"
Mentee: "...oh, it's from the async call above. I bet I'm not waiting
         for it to resolve."
Mentor: "Try it and see."
[Mentee learns a repeatable debugging method: read the error, find the
 stack frame, trace the value backward. Next time they can do this alone.]

      Real-World Scenario at Scale (large team / org)

      At scale

      In larger orgs, mentoring often gets formalized into a program (assigned mentor-mentee pairs, a syllabus, check-in forms) because ad-hoc mentoring doesn't reach everyone evenly — people who are already confident and visible tend to get informal mentoring for free, while quieter or newer-to-the-industry engineers get missed. A formal program's main risk is becoming a box-checking exercise (forms filled out, no real relationship); the fix is giving mentors real guidance (like the rungs of help above) rather than just pairing people and hoping, and making it genuinely optional to continue a pairing that isn't working rather than forcing a mismatch to persist for the program's sake.

      Common Mistakes, Gotchas and Best Practices

      Gotcha

      Doing a mentee's hard thinking for them because it's faster and you're under deadline pressure yourself. It feels efficient in the moment and is a net loss within a month — they bring you the same category of problem again, and now you've also taught them that bringing you problems gets them solved for free, which increases how often they do it.

      • Mistake: giving only corrective feedback and never naming what's going well — people can't tell what to repeat if you only ever flag what to fix.
      • Mistake: assigning stretch work with no safety net and then disappearing — the "stretch" part requires real risk of struggle, but abandonment isn't mentoring, it's neglect with a growth-mindset label on it.
      • Best practice: ask permission before giving feedback in the moment ("mind if I share a thought on that approach?") — it respects their agency and makes the feedback land as collaborative rather than imposed.
      • Best practice: celebrate the moment a mentee solves something you used to have to solve for them — say it out loud ("you didn't need me for that one") so they notice their own growth, which they often don't track themselves.

      Interview Questions

      Basic

      What's the difference between mentoring and just answering someone's questions?

      Mentoring aims to build the mentee's independent ability to solve the next similar problem; just answering questions solves today's problem but doesn't transfer a reusable method, so the same category of question tends to recur.

      Follow-up: how do you check whether you actually taught a transferable skill?
      Describe the "ladder of help" from least to most hand-holding.

      Ask a clarifying question, point at where to look, hint at the mechanism, pair and narrate your reasoning, and finally just give the answer — reserved for emergencies or non-transferable quirks. Pick the rung based on the person's level and the situation.

      Follow-up: what happens if you always default to the bottom rung (just give the answer)?
      Why is regular 1:1 time valuable even in weeks with "nothing specific" to discuss?

      The regularity itself signals the mentee matters and provides a natural checkpoint for smaller issues to surface before they become bigger ones; skipping "quiet" weeks tends to mean the relationship quietly fades.

      Follow-up: what would you actually talk about in a quiet week?
      What's a stretch assignment, and why give one?

      A task deliberately slightly beyond the mentee's current comfort zone, with the mentor available as a safety net — it builds capability faster than only giving well-trodden, safe tickets, because real growth happens near the edge of competence.

      Follow-up: what makes a stretch assignment go wrong?
      Why should feedback include both positive and corrective comments?

      People need to know what to repeat as much as what to fix — only ever giving corrective feedback leaves them unable to tell which of their behaviors are actually working.

      Follow-up: how do you avoid positive feedback feeling generic or empty?
      Why is "give them the answer" sometimes the right call despite the usual advice to teach?

      During an active incident or when the issue is a non-transferable tool quirk, speed matters more than the learning opportunity, and there's no meaningful skill being built by making them discover it themselves.

      Follow-up: how do you tell the difference in the moment?

      Medium

      A mentee keeps bringing you the same type of bug to fix instead of debugging it themselves. What do you do?

      Recognize you've likely been giving answers instead of teaching method on this category of problem. Shift explicitly: next time, walk them through your own debugging process out loud (read error, find stack frame, trace backward) instead of diagnosing it yourself, and name that you're doing this intentionally so they understand the shift.

      Follow-up: how do you know when they've actually internalized the method versus just memorized this specific fix?
      How do you mentor someone who's plateaued at a solid-but-not-growing level?

      Diagnose the cause specifically — often it's lack of stretch assignments (too comfortable) or a quiet skills gap they're avoiding (e.g., avoiding incident leadership or public-facing work). Name it directly rather than hoping they self-diagnose, and co-design a specific growth area and assignment rather than vague encouragement to "push yourself."

      Follow-up: what if they push back and say they're happy where they are?
      How do you give a mentee corrective feedback without demotivating them?

      Ask permission before giving it in the moment, be specific about the behavior (not the person), pair it with something genuinely going well, and focus on the next action rather than dwelling on the mistake itself. Timing matters too — close to the event, not saved up and delivered in a batch during a review.

      Follow-up: what if the same corrective feedback needs repeating multiple times?
      You're mentoring someone outside your reporting line. How does that change your approach?

      Feedback has to be framed more explicitly as optional guidance rather than a directive, since you can't back it with a performance review or formal authority. Being extra clear that it's "take it or leave it, your call" preserves the relationship and avoids it feeling like unwelcome oversight from someone with no formal standing to give it.

      Follow-up: how do you handle it if they consistently ignore your advice?
      How do you know when a mentee no longer needs a particular kind of mentoring from you?

      Track whether they can solve the next similar problem alone — if yes repeatedly across a category, that mentoring need has graduated; shift to a new growth area or to a more peer-like collaboration on that dimension. Explicitly naming the graduation out loud helps them notice their own growth, which they often underestimate.

      Follow-up: what if you keep treating them as needing help in an area they've already outgrown?

      Advanced

      Tell me about a time you mentored someone and it didn't go well. What did you learn?

      (Scenario-based — strong answers name a specific mismatch, e.g. giving answers instead of teaching method under deadline pressure, or a stretch assignment given with insufficient safety net that led to a real production issue, and describe a concrete change made afterward — like instituting mid-point check-ins on stretch work, or explicitly naming the mentoring approach being used so it doesn't feel arbitrary.)

      Follow-up: how did you repair trust with that person afterward?
      How do you scale mentoring when you have 4-5 mentees simultaneously without diluting quality for any of them?

      Shift from "I personally solve every problem they bring" to building systems that scale: a shared FAQ/runbook for common questions, pairing mentees with each other on problems you've already seen (peer teaching reinforces your own mentee's learning too), and reserving your direct 1:1 time for the things only you can provide — judgment calls, stretch-assignment selection, and feedback that requires your specific context.

      Follow-up: how do you avoid any one mentee feeling like they're getting leftover attention?
      Describe mentoring someone who is more senior than you in some other dimension (e.g., a domain expert junior in tenure but senior in a specific area). How do you approach it?

      Reframe it explicitly as mutual — you mentor on the dimensions where you have more context (e.g., org navigation, review norms, incident process) and genuinely learn from them on their area of expertise. Being transparent about this two-way structure avoids it feeling condescending, which it would if you tried to mentor uniformly across a dimension where they actually know more than you.

      Follow-up: how do you handle it if they're uncomfortable with the mentor label given the mutual dynamic?
      How would you design a mentoring program for a 100-person engineering org where mentoring currently happens ad-hoc and unevenly?

      Formalize lightly: opt-in pairing (not forced matches), a short shared syllabus/rung-of-help guide so mentors without natural teaching instincts have something concrete to follow, a low-stakes check-in cadence (quarterly, not heavy forms), and an explicit, blameless way to end a pairing that isn't working. Track participation and a lagging signal like promotion/retention rates among participants vs non-participants rather than just "number of pairs formed," which can be gamed with low-quality matches.

      Follow-up: how do you prevent the program from just formalizing the existing uneven access — i.e., the same confident people opting in while quieter engineers still get missed?
      A mentee you've invested heavily in is being recruited away by another team or company. How do you handle the conversation, and does it change how you mentor going forward?

      Support their growth even if it means losing them — mentoring someone to leave well is still a success, not a failure, and treating it otherwise (guilt-tripping, subtly discouraging the move) damages trust with every future mentee who hears about it. It doesn't change the fundamentals of how you mentor, but it's worth being honest with yourself about whether you mentor partly to retain talent on your own team versus to grow people, since the two motivations can quietly diverge.

      Follow-up: how do you handle your own feelings of loss or being "used" if this happens repeatedly?

      Practice Assignments

      Beginner (30-60 min)

      Rewrite a "just gave the answer" conversation

      Pick a recent real conversation where you (or someone you observed) immediately gave an answer to a junior's question. Rewrite it as a Socratic dialogue using the ladder of help, climbing from clarifying question up to pairing if needed.

      • At least 3 exchanges before the mentee reaches the answer themselves.
      • Name which rung of the ladder each exchange uses.

      Acceptance criteria: The mentee in your rewritten dialogue reaches the answer through their own reasoning, not by being told.

      Hints

      Start the dialogue with "what have you tried?" or "what did you expect vs what happened?" — these two questions alone surface most of the gap.

      Bonus: Write the mentor's internal reasoning for why they picked that rung at each step.

      Intermediate (2-4 hrs)

      Design a 90-day growth plan

      Pick a realistic junior/mid-level profile (invent one with specific current strengths and gaps) and write a 90-day growth plan for mentoring them.

      • One named growth area with a concrete, observable definition of success.
      • At least 2 stretch assignments with increasing difficulty, each with a stated safety net.
      • A 1:1 cadence and what gets discussed at each checkpoint (day 30, 60, 90).

      Acceptance criteria: A third party reading the plan could tell, at day 90, whether it succeeded or not without you having to interpret it for them.

      Hints

      "Got better at communication" is not observable. "Led a customer-facing incident call without the mentor present" is.

      Bonus: Write the plan for a mentee outside your reporting line and note what changes about the feedback framing.

      Advanced (1-2 days)

      Design a mentoring program for a scaling org

      A 100-person engineering org has grown from 20 people in two years. Mentoring used to happen naturally because everyone knew everyone; now new hires report feeling lost and senior engineers report feeling stretched too thin to mentor informally. Design a lightweight formal program.

      • Matching mechanism (opt-in, assigned, or hybrid) with reasoning for your choice.
      • A syllabus or guide mentors without strong teaching instincts can follow.
      • A way to measure whether the program is actually working after 2 quarters.
      • A way to end a bad pairing gracefully.
      Hints (no full solution)

      Think hard about failure modes of formal mentoring programs you've seen or heard of — box-checking, uneven mentor quality, forced pairs — and design against each one specifically rather than generically.

      Quick Revision Cheat Sheet

      • Goal: they can solve the next similar problem alone, not just this one.
      • Ladder of help: question → point → hint → pair → give the answer (reserve for emergencies).
      • After helping, ask: "could they do the next one alone now?"
      • Structure beats ad-hoc: named growth area, regular 1:1, stretch assignments, timely feedback.
      • Stretch assignments need a real safety net, not abandonment.
      • Give positive feedback too — people need to know what to repeat.
      • Ask permission before in-the-moment feedback; respects agency.
      • Plateaued mentee: name the specific cause, don't just encourage vaguely.
      • Mentoring outside your reporting line: frame feedback as optional, you have no formal lever.
      • Celebrate when they outgrow needing you for something — say it out loud.
