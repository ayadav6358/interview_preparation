    Incident Handling and Post-Mortems

    ⏱ ~3 hrs📍 Chapter 6 of 6

    One-line summary: This chapter goes deep on how production incidents are actually classified, commanded, communicated, and learned from at a senior level — severity taxonomies, on-call escalation mechanics, the incident commander role during a live outage, and how to run a blameless post-mortem that produces action items which actually get done.

    On this page

      Why It Matters for a Senior Developer

      Anyone can write code that works most of the time; what separates a senior/staff engineer is how they behave in the 2am window when it doesn't. Interviewers probe this because it's one of the few scenarios that reveals judgment under pressure, communication discipline, and systems thinking simultaneously — "tell me about a production incident you handled" is asked specifically to see whether you can stay calm, triage correctly, communicate status without causing panic, and extract a real structural fix afterward rather than a superficial one. Teams that handle incidents well don't have fewer outages than everyone else — they have faster detection, faster mitigation, and a post-mortem process that actually reduces repeat incidents, which is a measurable, interview-relevant signal of engineering maturity.

      Prerequisites

      Logging, metrics and alerting fundamentals, and basic production operations. Related: see the Observability & Security topic overview, and especially the chapters on logging, metrics and tracing and alerting, SLOs and SLIs for the detection layer that feeds into everything below, plus secure auth & secrets for the kind of security incident this process also has to cover.

      Core Concepts

      Severity classification: SEV1 through SEV4

      Every mature org classifies incidents by a severity level the moment they're declared, because severity determines who gets paged, how loudly, and how fast. A practical, widely-used taxonomy:

      SEV1 (Critical): complete outage or major data-integrity/security breach affecting all or most users — site down, payments failing for everyone, customer data exposed. Requires immediate all-hands response, an incident commander, and typically executive/customer communication within minutes. SEV2 (High): significant degradation or a major feature broken for a large subset of users — checkout works but is slow for 30% of traffic, search is down but browsing works. Urgent response, but not necessarily a company-wide page. SEV3 (Medium): a minor feature broken or degraded affecting a small subset of users or an internal tool — handled during business hours by the owning team, tracked but not paged. SEV4 (Low): cosmetic issue, edge case, or a problem with an easy workaround — logged as a ticket, no incident process invoked. The senior-level point: severity is about user/business impact, not about how technically interesting or scary the underlying bug is — a terrifying-looking stack trace that affects zero real users is a SEV4, and a boring off-by-one that silently drops 10% of payment webhooks is a SEV1.

      On-call mechanics and escalation

      An on-call rotation (via PagerDuty, Opsgenie, or similar) defines who gets paged first for a given service, with a defined escalation policy: if the primary on-call doesn't acknowledge within N minutes (typically 5-15), the page automatically escalates to a secondary, then to the team lead, then to a broader engineering-management chain. This isn't bureaucracy — it's a safety net against the very real failure mode of "the one person who knows this system is asleep/on a plane/has a dead phone." A good on-call setup also has clear paging criteria tied to SLO burn-rate alerts (see the alerting/SLO chapter) rather than raw threshold alerts, so people are paged for symptoms that matter to users, not for every noisy metric blip — alert fatigue from over-paging is one of the most common root causes of a slow SEV1 response, because the on-call engineer has learned to triage pages by ignoring them.

      Incident command roles during a live SEV1/SEV2

      Borrowed from FEMA's Incident Command System and adapted by Google/PagerDuty-style SRE practice, a live major incident needs explicit roles, not an ad-hoc scramble: Incident Commander (IC) — owns the overall response, makes the call on severity/escalation/when it's resolved, and explicitly does not personally debug the issue; their job is coordination, not root-causing. Operations Lead / Subject Matter Expert — the engineer(s) actually in the code/infra fixing it. Communications Lead — owns the status page, Slack incident channel updates, and customer/stakeholder messaging on a fixed cadence (e.g., every 15-30 minutes even if the update is "still investigating"), so the IC and SMEs are never interrupted by "any updates?" pings. Scribe — keeps a real-time timeline of what was tried, when, and what happened, which becomes the raw material for the post-mortem. The core senior insight: separating "who's fixing it" from "who's deciding/communicating" is what prevents a SEV1 from degenerating into five engineers simultaneously debugging in isolation while nobody tells customer support what to tell customers.

      Blameless post-mortem structure

      A post-mortem is written within 1-3 business days after resolution, while memory is fresh, and follows a fixed structure: Summary (one paragraph: what broke, impact, duration). Timeline (timestamped, factual: "14:02 — deploy of v2.3.1 began," "14:07 — error rate alert fired," "14:11 — on-call acknowledged," "14:19 — rollback initiated," "14:24 — error rate normal"). Root cause, found via the 5 whys technique: ask "why did this happen" five times (or until you hit a structural/process answer, not necessarily exactly 5), e.g. "Why did checkout fail?" → "A null pointer in the pricing service." → "Why was it null?" → "A new discount field wasn't populated for legacy SKUs." → "Why wasn't it populated?" → "The migration script skipped SKUs created before 2023." → "Why did the migration skip them?" → "It filtered on a 'created_at' column that didn't exist on the oldest rows." → "Why did nobody catch this in testing?" → "Staging data was seeded only with post-2023 SKUs" — the real root cause is the last one (staging data doesn't represent production data shape), not the null pointer. Contributing factors (things that made it worse or slower to detect/fix, distinct from the root cause — e.g., "the relevant dashboard didn't have an alert on this metric," "the runbook was out of date"). Action items, each with an explicit owner and due date, categorized by whether they prevent recurrence, improve detection, or improve response speed — a post-mortem with no concrete, owned action items is a story, not a process improvement.

      Why "blameless" is a mechanism, not a slogan

      Blameless doesn't mean "nobody is responsible" — it means the write-up and discussion focus on systemic and process causes rather than individual fault, because the senior-level recognition is that a competent engineer who followed the documented process and still caused an outage is evidence the process is broken, not that the engineer is incompetent. The practical reason this matters beyond psychological safety: if post-mortems assign blame, the incentive shifts to hiding information, hedging language in the timeline, and avoiding volunteering for risky deploys/on-call — which directly degrades the quality of the data you need to actually prevent recurrence. A post-mortem that names "the deploy script allowed a migration to run without a dry-run gate" produces a fixable action item; one that names "Priya should have been more careful" produces nothing actionable and discourages the next person from being honest about their own near-misses.

      How It Works Under the Hood (In Practice)

      In a mature org, the mechanics behind the roles above are concrete tooling, not just culture: a paging system (PagerDuty/Opsgenie) integrates with alerting (Datadog/Prometheus Alertmanager/CloudWatch) so an SLO burn-rate breach fires a page with a deep link to the relevant dashboard and runbook already attached. Declaring an incident is usually a single slash-command (e.g., /incident declare in Slack) that auto-creates a dedicated incident channel, pulls in a bot that posts the timeline as people react with emoji/commands, creates a shared doc pre-populated with the post-mortem template, and opens an entry on the public status page if the severity crosses a threshold. The IC role is often assigned by whoever declares the incident by default, with an explicit hand-off if a more senior IC needs to take over — the hand-off itself gets a timeline entry ("14:31 — IC handed off from Alice to Bob") because continuity of who's in charge matters as much as the technical fix. The timeline the scribe keeps is the single most valuable artifact for the post-mortem — teams that skip live timeline-keeping and try to reconstruct it from memory afterward consistently produce worse post-mortems, because exact sequencing (what was tried before the actual fix, and why those earlier things didn't work) gets lost.

      # Post-Mortem Template (Blameless)

## Summary
One paragraph: what broke, who/what was impacted, how long, how it was detected and resolved.

## Severity & Impact
- Severity: SEV1 / SEV2 / SEV3 / SEV4
- Duration: start timestamp -> resolved timestamp (total minutes)
- User impact: e.g. "12% of checkout requests returned 500 for 34 minutes"
- Business impact: e.g. estimated revenue impact, SLA credit exposure

## Timeline (all timestamps in UTC)
| Time  | Event |
|-------|-------|
| 14:02 | Deploy of payments-service v2.3.1 began |
| 14:07 | Error-rate SLO burn-rate alert fired, paged on-call |
| 14:11 | On-call acknowledged, began investigating logs |
| 14:15 | Identified correlation with the 14:02 deploy |
| 14:19 | Rollback to v2.3.0 initiated |
| 14:24 | Error rate returned to baseline |
| 14:30 | Incident declared resolved |

## Root Cause (5 Whys)
1. Why did checkout fail? -> Null pointer exception in discount calculation.
2. Why was the discount field null? -> Migration didn't backfill legacy SKUs.
3. Why didn't the migration backfill them? -> It filtered on a column absent on pre-2023 rows.
4. Why wasn't this caught in staging? -> Staging seed data only contains post-2023 SKUs.
5. Why does staging data not represent production? -> No process requires staging data to be
   periodically refreshed from an anonymized production snapshot.

**Root cause:** Staging environment data does not represent the shape/age distribution of
production data, so schema-dependent bugs on old rows are undetectable before deploy.

## Contributing Factors
- No alert existed specifically on discount-calculation error rate; detection relied on the
  generic checkout error-rate SLO, delaying root-cause identification by ~4 minutes.
- The rollback runbook was last updated 6 months ago and referenced a deprecated deploy tool.

## Action Items
| Action | Owner | Due | Category |
|--------|-------|-----|----------|
| Refresh staging data from anonymized prod snapshot monthly | @dana | 2026-10-20 | Prevention |
| Add schema-null-check alert on discount field specifically | @raj | 2026-10-10 | Detection |
| Update rollback runbook to current deploy tooling | @sam | 2026-10-08 | Response speed |
| Add migration dry-run gate requiring row-count diff review | @dana | 2026-10-24 | Prevention |

## What Went Well
- Burn-rate alert fired within 5 minutes of the regression starting.
- Rollback was executed within 8 minutes of root-cause identification.

      // =========================================================================
// Incident comms: BAD vs GOOD status updates during a live SEV1
// =========================================================================

// BAD — vague, no timestamp, implies certainty that doesn't exist yet,
// gives no indication of when the next update will come (forces people
// to keep pinging the incident channel for status, interrupting the fix).
const badUpdate = `We're looking into some issues. Should be fixed soon.`;

// GOOD — timestamped, states current understanding without overclaiming,
// states concrete impact, and commits to the next update time so nobody
// needs to ask "any news?" in the meantime.
function postStatusUpdate(params: {
  timestampUtc: string;
  impact: string;
  currentAction: string;
  nextUpdateInMinutes: number;
}) {
  return (
    `[${params.timestampUtc} UTC] Investigating degraded checkout performance ` +
    `affecting an estimated 15% of requests. Current theory: a recent deploy ` +
    `increased database query latency. Rolling back the suspect deploy now. ` +
    `Next update in ${params.nextUpdateInMinutes} minutes or sooner if status changes.`
  );
}

// =========================================================================
// 5 Whys as a structured helper — forces explicit "why" chaining instead
// of jumping straight to the first proximate cause (the common mistake).
// =========================================================================
interface WhyStep {
  question: string;
  answer: string;
}

function fiveWhys(symptom: string, chain: WhyStep[]): string {
  if (chain.length < 3) {
    throw new Error(
      "Root-cause analysis stopped too early — fewer than 3 'whys' usually " +
      "means you've found a proximate cause, not a structural root cause."
    );
  }
  const lastAnswer = chain[chain.length - 1].answer;
  return `Symptom: ${symptom}\nRoot cause: ${lastAnswer}`;
}

// =========================================================================
// Escalation policy modeled explicitly — the actual mechanism that
// prevents "nobody saw the page" from stalling a SEV1 response.
// =========================================================================
interface EscalationStep {
  target: string;
  ackTimeoutMinutes: number;
}

async function pageWithEscalation(
  steps: EscalationStep[],
  sendPage: (target: string) => Promise,
  waitForAck: (target: string, timeoutMs: number) => Promise
): Promise {
  for (const step of steps) {
    await sendPage(step.target);
    const acked = await waitForAck(step.target, step.ackTimeoutMinutes * 60_000);
    if (acked) return step.target; // this person/team owns the response now
  }
  throw new Error("Escalation policy exhausted with no acknowledgment — page a human directly.");
}

      Real-World Production Scenario at Scale

      At scale

      An e-commerce platform's payment webhook processor silently dropped ~8% of webhooks during a database failover, because the retry logic only retried on HTTP 5xx and the failover produced a brief run of connection-reset errors that the client library surfaced as a different exception type, which the retry wrapper didn't catch. No alert fired, because the error rate on the webhook endpoint itself stayed low (the drops happened before the handler even executed) — it was only discovered three weeks later when finance reconciliation flagged a mismatch between expected and recorded payments. The incident was retroactively classified SEV2 (real financial impact, but not customer-facing/time-critical once understood), and the post-mortem's 5-whys traced it past "retry logic didn't catch this exception type" to the actual structural root cause: there was no end-to-end reconciliation check comparing upstream-sent vs. downstream-received event counts, so any silent drop — regardless of its specific cause — would have gone undetected for an arbitrary length of time. The highest-leverage action item wasn't fixing that one retry bug (which only prevents that exact recurrence); it was adding a continuous reconciliation job, which would catch an entire class of future silent-drop bugs regardless of cause.

      Common Mistakes, Gotchas and Best Practices

      Gotcha

      Stopping the 5-whys at the first technical answer (e.g., "a null pointer") and writing that as the root cause produces an action item that only prevents that exact bug from recurring, not the class of bugs it belongs to. A root cause stated as "staging data didn't represent production data shape" is reusable — it informs a prevention strategy for every future schema-dependent bug, not just this one. If your 5-whys chain stops after two "whys," you almost certainly haven't reached a structural cause yet.

      Gotcha

      Conflating the Incident Commander role with "the most senior engineer" or "whoever's debugging it" is a common and costly mistake — the IC's job is coordination and decision-making, not hands-on-keyboard debugging, and an IC who also tries to be the one fixing the code loses the ability to make clear-headed calls about escalation, rollback-vs-forward-fix, and customer communication timing. On a real SEV1, the most technically capable person is often better used as the Operations Lead while someone else — even someone less senior — runs the IC role, as long as they're trained in it.

      • Writing a post-mortem that reads like a confession rather than a systems analysis invites blame culture and makes future incidents less likely to be reported honestly or quickly.
      • Action items with no owner or due date are, in practice, never completed — track them in the same system as regular engineering work (sprint board, not a forgotten doc), and review completion rate as a team metric.
      • Classifying severity by how scary the error message looks rather than by actual user/business impact leads to either under-reacting to a quiet-but-severe data-corruption bug or over-reacting to a loud but harmless one.
      • Skipping a live, timestamped timeline during the incident and trying to reconstruct it afterward from memory and Slack scrollback produces a materially worse post-mortem — assign a scribe from the start of any SEV1/SEV2.

      Interview Questions

      Basic

      What determines an incident's severity level — the technical scariness of the bug or something else?

      User and business impact — scope of affected users, revenue/data/compliance exposure, and urgency — not how alarming the underlying stack trace or bug looks technically.

      Follow-up: give an example of a technically trivial bug that would still be SEV1.
      What's the difference between SEV1 and SEV3?

      SEV1 is a complete outage or major breach affecting most/all users requiring an immediate all-hands incident response; SEV3 is a minor feature degradation affecting a small subset, handled by the owning team during business hours without invoking the full incident process.

      Follow-up: who typically decides the severity, and when during the response?
      What does "blameless" mean in a blameless post-mortem?

      The analysis and write-up focus on systemic and process causes rather than individual fault — the goal is extracting fixable structural causes, not assigning blame, because blame-focused post-mortems discourage honest reporting and hide the information needed to actually prevent recurrence.

      Follow-up: why does blame-assignment specifically degrade the quality of future incident data?
      What is the 5 whys technique?

      Repeatedly asking "why did this happen" starting from the symptom, chaining each answer into the next question, until you reach a structural/process-level root cause rather than stopping at the first proximate technical cause.

      Follow-up: how do you know when you've reached the "real" root cause versus stopping too early?
      What is an escalation policy in an on-call system?

      A defined chain of who gets paged next if the current person doesn't acknowledge within a set time window — primary, then secondary, then team lead, etc. — so an unresponsive on-call engineer doesn't stall the response indefinitely.

      Follow-up: what's a reasonable ack timeout, and why not make it very short?
      Why should action items from a post-mortem have an explicit owner and due date?

      Without an owner and date, action items are, in practice, never completed — they need to be tracked like regular engineering work, not left in a static document nobody revisits.

      Follow-up: how would you track completion rate as a team-level metric?
      What's the role of the Incident Commander, and what do they explicitly NOT do?

      The IC owns coordination, severity/escalation decisions, and declaring resolution; they explicitly do not personally debug the code — that's the Operations Lead/SME's job — so the IC can keep a clear head for decision-making.

      Follow-up: why is combining the IC and SME roles risky on a real SEV1?

      Medium

      Walk through why a post-mortem's root cause should usually be a process/structural statement rather than a single line of buggy code.

      A root cause stated as a specific code bug ("null pointer on line 42") only prevents that exact recurrence; chaining the 5 whys further to something like "staging data doesn't represent production data shape" or "no dry-run gate exists for migrations" identifies a class of future bugs the same gap would also cause, making the resulting action item far higher-leverage.

      Follow-up: how do you decide when you've gone "far enough" and not kept whying into generic organizational issues unrelated to this incident?
      Why separate the Communications Lead role from the engineers actually fixing the issue during a SEV1?

      Without a dedicated comms lead, SMEs and the IC get repeatedly interrupted by "any updates?" pings from stakeholders/support/customers, which slows the actual fix; a comms lead posting on a fixed cadence (e.g., every 15-30 min) absorbs that need independently, letting the technical responders stay focused.

      Follow-up: what should a status update communicate even when there's no real progress to report?
      A recurring SEV2 keeps happening every few months with slightly different proximate causes each time. What does that pattern suggest about the post-mortem process itself?

      If the proximate cause differs but the incident recurs, the previous post-mortems likely stopped the 5-whys too early and fixed only the specific trigger each time rather than the shared structural weakness enabling all of them (e.g., a missing class of test coverage, a fragile shared dependency, or an organizational gap like no ownership of a particular pipeline) — the right move is a dedicated cross-incident review looking at all the post-mortems together for the common thread.

      Follow-up: how would you present this pattern to leadership to get investment in the real structural fix?
      How should severity classification interact with SLO burn-rate alerting discussed in the alerting chapter?

      Paging criteria should be tied to SLO burn-rate (how fast you're consuming error budget) rather than raw threshold breaches, so severity roughly tracks how quickly the situation threatens to exhaust the error budget / breach the SLA — a slow burn might be a SEV3 ticket, a fast burn consuming a month's error budget in an hour is a SEV1 page regardless of the absolute error count being technically small.

      Follow-up: why is raw error-count threshold alerting prone to both false positives and dangerously late true positives compared to burn-rate alerting?
      Why might a less senior engineer sometimes be a better Incident Commander than the most senior engineer available during a SEV1?

      IC is a coordination and communication skill distinct from technical debugging skill — a strong IC stays calm, delegates clearly, manages comms cadence, and makes timely escalation/rollback calls without needing to personally understand every line of the failing code; the most senior engineer is often more valuable heads-down as the Operations Lead actually fixing the issue, and trying to do both roles at once degrades both.

      Follow-up: what training or practice would you put in place so more engineers are comfortable taking the IC role?

      Advanced

      Design an incident response program from scratch for a 40-engineer company that currently has no formal process (outages get fixed ad hoc, no post-mortems). What do you build first, and in what order?

      First: a severity taxonomy (SEV1-4) with clear impact-based criteria, because nothing else works without a shared definition of "how bad is this." Second: an on-call rotation and escalation policy per service with paging tied to existing alerts, even if those alerts are imperfect initially. Third: a lightweight incident-declaration mechanism (even a Slack command/channel convention) that assigns IC/Comms/Scribe roles for SEV1/SEV2 only at first — don't try to formalize SEV3/4 yet. Fourth: a blameless post-mortem template and a recurring review meeting where action items are tracked to completion as a visible team metric. Only after these are running smoothly for a few incidents would you invest in deeper tooling (status pages, automated timeline bots, SLO burn-rate paging) — process and culture come before tooling, because good tooling on top of a blame-driven or inconsistent process doesn't fix the underlying dysfunction.

      Follow-up: how would you get buy-in from engineers who see this as "process overhead" after years of ad hoc firefighting?
      Critique: "Every incident needs a full post-mortem meeting with the whole team, or we're not learning from it." What's wrong with this as a blanket policy?

      Mandating a full synchronous meeting for every incident, including low-severity recurring ones with already-understood causes, creates meeting fatigue that erodes the perceived value of the process and discourages timely incident declaration for smaller issues (engineers under-report to avoid triggering overhead). The senior approach scales the process to severity: SEV1/SEV2 get a full written post-mortem and a review meeting; SEV3 gets a lightweight written note with action items tracked but no mandatory meeting, with periodic batch reviews looking for patterns across several SEV3s; SEV4 gets a ticket. The goal is consistent learning capture at low overhead, not uniform ceremony regardless of severity.

      Follow-up: how would you still catch a dangerous pattern hiding across many individually-minor SEV3/4 incidents if they don't get full review?
      How do you handle a post-mortem when the root cause traces back to a decision made by a team or individual who is no longer at the company, or to a vendor's internal bug you have no visibility into?

      For a departed-employee cause: focus entirely on the current-state structural gap (e.g., "this decision was undocumented and un-reviewed, which is why nobody caught it") rather than the absent person, and the action item becomes a process fix (mandatory design review, documentation requirement) rather than anything about the individual. For a vendor's internal bug: the 5-whys should stop at your own boundary of control — the real actionable root cause on your side is usually "we had no fallback/circuit-breaker for this dependency" or "we had no contractual SLA/monitoring on this vendor's behavior," since you can't fix their code, but you can fix your blast-radius exposure to their failures.

      Follow-up: how would you incorporate vendor incident post-mortems (if they publish one) into your own action items?
      Your org measures incident response success primarily by MTTR (mean time to resolution). What perverse incentives can this metric create, and how would you counter them?

      Optimizing purely for MTTR incentivizes quick-but-fragile mitigations (e.g., a rollback that papers over data inconsistency, or silencing an alert instead of fixing the underlying issue) because "resolved" gets measured the instant symptoms disappear, not whether the fix is durable or whether it created a slower-burning secondary problem. It also discourages thorough root-causing, since time spent understanding the real cause before mitigating looks bad on the clock. Counter with a balanced scorecard: track MTTR alongside repeat-incident rate for the same root cause, percentage of post-mortems with completed action items, and a qualitative review of whether the mitigation was a durable fix vs. a stopgap — and explicitly normalize "we took 10 extra minutes to understand this correctly before acting" as good behavior, not something MTTR alone would reward.

      Follow-up: what's a leading indicator (not just MTTR/MTTD) that predicts incident response quality before the next incident even happens?
      How would you design the handoff process for a long-running SEV1 that spans a shift change or multiple time zones, without losing context or accountability?

      Treat IC handoff as a first-class, explicitly logged event in the timeline (who, when, confirmed acknowledgment from the incoming IC), paired with a mandatory structured handoff summary covering: current working theory, what's been tried and ruled out, current mitigation status, and the next planned action — delivered synchronously (a call, not just a Slack message dump) so the incoming IC can ask clarifying questions before taking over. The outgoing IC remains reachable for a defined window (e.g., 30 minutes) in case the incoming IC needs clarification. The scribe's running timeline is the single source of truth that makes this possible — without it, every handoff re-derives context from scratch and risks repeating already-ruled-out mitigation attempts.

      Follow-up: what's the risk of NOT having a defined overlap window between outgoing and incoming IC?

      Practice Assignments

      Beginner (30-60 min)

      Write a severity-classification rubric and classify sample incidents

      Write a one-page SEV1-4 rubric with concrete, impact-based criteria (not technical-severity-based) for a hypothetical e-commerce app. Then classify five short incident scenarios you write yourself (e.g., "checkout 500s for all users for 10 minutes," "a typo in a marketing banner," "an internal admin tool is slow for 3 employees," "a data export silently drops 2% of rows for a week before detection") against your rubric.

      • Each SEV level in your rubric must state a concrete, measurable criterion, not a vague description.
      • For each sample scenario, write one sentence justifying the severity you assigned.

      Acceptance criteria: A rubric document plus five classified scenarios with justification sentences.

      Hints

      Base your criteria on affected-user percentage, revenue/data/compliance exposure, and whether a workaround exists — not on how scary the bug sounds.

      Bonus: Add a column for "who gets paged" at each severity level.

      Intermediate (2-4 hrs)

      Run a simulated incident and write a full post-mortem

      Pick (or recall) a real bug you've fixed before, or construct a plausible one in a toy app. Simulate an incident timeline for it (write out realistic timestamps for detection, acknowledgment, investigation, and mitigation), then write a complete post-mortem using the template from this chapter's code examples, including a genuine 5-whys chain that goes at least 4 levels deep and reaches a structural root cause, not a single line of code.

      • Timeline must have at least 6 distinct timestamped events.
      • 5-whys chain must have at least 4 "why" steps and end in a process/structural statement.
      • At least 3 action items, each with an owner (can be a role, e.g. "on-call lead") and a due date.

      Acceptance criteria: A complete post-mortem document following the template, reviewed against the checklist above.

      Hints

      If your 5-whys chain stops at "a bug in the code," you've stopped too early — ask why that specific bug wasn't caught by tests, review, or staging, and why that gap exists.

      Bonus: Add a "contributing factors" section distinct from the root cause.

      Advanced (1-2 days)

      Design and tabletop-exercise a full incident response program

      Design an end-to-end incident response program for a hypothetical 50-person engineering org with no existing process: severity taxonomy, on-call rotation and escalation policy design, IC/Comms/Scribe role definitions and training plan, a post-mortem template and review cadence, and a tracking mechanism for action-item completion as a team metric. Then run a tabletop exercise: write a realistic SEV1 scenario and simulate the full response end-to-end on paper — who gets paged, who declares the incident, who takes each role, what the comms updates say at each interval, and the resulting post-mortem.

      • Program design must address at least 3 time zones / follow-the-sun on-call handoff.
      • Tabletop must include at least one escalation (an initial page going unacknowledged).
      • Tabletop must include at least one IC handoff mid-incident with a structured handoff summary.
      Hints

      This is intentionally open-ended — think about what breaks the process under realistic friction (someone's asleep, a key person is on vacation, two incidents happen simultaneously) rather than designing only for the clean-path case.

      Bonus: Add a cross-incident review step that looks for a recurring root-cause pattern across multiple post-mortems.

      Quick Revision Cheat Sheet

      • Severity (SEV1-4) is driven by user/business impact, not how scary the bug looks technically.
      • On-call escalation policies auto-escalate an unacknowledged page to a secondary/lead — a safety net against one unreachable person stalling response.
      • Incident Commander coordinates and decides; they do not personally debug — that's the Operations Lead/SME's job.
      • A Comms Lead posts updates on a fixed cadence so SMEs aren't interrupted by status-check pings.
      • A Scribe's live, timestamped timeline is the single most valuable post-mortem input — don't reconstruct it from memory afterward.
      • 5 whys: keep chaining past the first proximate technical cause until you reach a structural/process-level root cause.
      • Contributing factors are distinct from root cause — they made things worse/slower, but aren't "why it happened."
      • Action items need an explicit owner and due date, tracked like normal engineering work, or they never get done.
      • Blameless means focusing on systemic causes, not because individuals don't matter, but because blame destroys the honest reporting the whole process depends on.
      • Scale post-mortem ceremony to severity — not every SEV3/4 needs a full meeting, but every SEV1/2 does.
